Week 14 · Social Studies & Civics

Informational Text and Argument Analysis

How can readers evaluate whether an argument is strong?

Monday — Read a historical-source scenario

Learn and model: Original Academy fictional classroom source: 'A merchant reports that a busy crossroads market offers cloth, pottery, and spices brought by travelers from distant towns. She complains that tolls have increased and says merchants now choose longer routes.' This is a constructed historical exercise, not an authentic period document. Trade networks linked people and goods but accounts of those networks reflect the experience and interests of their authors.

Practice and evidence: Underline observations about goods and routes. Circle claims about why merchants change routes. Explain why a single merchant may not represent all traders.

Tuesday — Analyze geography and incentives

Learn and model: Trade routes connect producers, consumers, and intermediaries. Mountains, deserts, seas, and political borders change travel costs and risks. A toll is a fee charged for using a route; a tariff typically applies to imported or exported goods. If a trader earns 50 units from sales, spends 20 transporting goods and 8 in tolls, the remaining amount before other costs is 22 units. Historical claims require chronology and source context.

Practice and evidence: Create a two-route comparison: Route A costs 20 for travel plus 8 in tolls; Route B costs 26 for travel plus 1 in tolls. Compute totals and explain why costs alone may not determine a choice.

Wednesday — Compare perspectives

Learn and model: A ruler might defend tolls as funding roads and security; a merchant might object to the effect on profit; a traveler may care most about safety. These are analytical perspectives, not evidence that every historical person believed the same thing. To investigate an actual period, seek dated primary sources and reliable scholarship, checking place, authorship, translation, and intended audience.

Practice and evidence: Write three short viewpoint statements and identify what additional historical evidence would be needed to confirm each one.

Thursday — Build an evidence-based interpretation

Learn and model: A convincing historical interpretation states an answer, identifies the source and its limitations, and connects evidence with reasoning. A fictional merchant narrative can practice these skills but cannot prove specific past events. Distinguish a historical hypothesis from a verified fact and acknowledge absent evidence.

Practice and evidence: Write 180–220 words evaluating the claim 'Higher tolls always reduce trade.' Use the scenario and arithmetic as illustrations; name two other variables and state why the evidence cannot support 'always.'

Friday — Historical reasoning portfolio

Learn and model: Present a route map (hand-drawn is acceptable), a cost comparison, and a paragraph explaining how geography, exchange, and government policy interact. Mark fictional data clearly and list the primary-source questions you would investigate for a real historical case.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20 points): conceptual accuracy 5; quantitative comparison 4; multiple perspectives 5; source limitations and clear argument 6. File the completed work in the existing portfolio; retain the separate weekly assessment.
