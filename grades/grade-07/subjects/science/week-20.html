Week 20 · Science & Engineering

Genetics Foundations and Variation

How do inheritance and environment shape living things?

Monday: Inherited traits and scientific vocabulary

Focus: heredity, DNA, genes, chromosomes, traits, and variation.

Learn: Organisms inherit genetic information from parents. DNA carries instructions, genes are sections of DNA, and chromosomes organize DNA inside cells. An inherited trait can include natural eye-color variation; a learned skill such as reading is not inherited as a ready-made ability. Genes influence many traits, and the environment also matters. Traits such as height are influenced by multiple genes and conditions such as nutrition.

Vocabulary: heredity—passing genetic information between generations; gene—a DNA segment involved in inherited information; chromosome—a DNA-containing structure; trait—a characteristic; variation—differences among individuals of a species.

Notebook practice: (1) Explain why siblings can differ. (2) Sort these into inherited, learned, or environmentally influenced: blood type, bicycle riding, suntan, natural hair texture. (3) Draw and label the relationship chromosome → DNA → gene; explain that a gene is a segment of DNA, not a separate substance.

Evidence: labeled diagram, vocabulary definitions in the scholar's own words, and two complete explanations.

Tuesday: Alleles and simple inheritance models

Focus: alleles, genotype, phenotype, dominant, and recessive.

Learn: An allele is a version of a gene. In a simplified model of a single-gene trait, a dominant allele A can be expressed in AA or Aa; a recessive allele a is expressed when the genotype is aa. A genotype names the allele combination; a phenotype describes the observed characteristic. Many real-world human traits do not follow this simple dominant/recessive pattern, so models must be used with care.

Worked model: Cross two fictional plants with genotype Aa. Each parent can contribute A or a. Fill a 2 × 2 Punnett square: AA, Aa, Aa, aa. The model predicts genotype proportions of 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa, and 1/4 aa, assuming the model conditions hold.

Practice: (1) Draw the Aa × Aa square. (2) For the fictional model, what fraction of offspring is aa? (3) Describe the difference between genotype and phenotype. (4) Why must we not assume that all human characteristics are controlled by one gene?

Evidence: completed square with labels, calculation, and limitations paragraph.

Wednesday: Investigation — variation in a fictional population

Focus: measuring variation and interpreting evidence.

Investigation data: A fictional population of ten plants has heights, in centimeters, of 12, 14, 14, 15, 16, 16, 17, 18, 19, and 19. These are simulated data, not a measured biological sample.

  1. Create a correctly labeled dot plot or frequency table.
  2. Find the minimum, maximum, and range.
  3. Calculate the mean height: sum all values, then divide by 10.
  4. Suggest two possible influences on height and explain why this sample alone cannot establish a genetic cause.
  5. Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph about whether the plants display variation.

Check: The range is 7 cm and the mean is 16 cm. The data show variation, but do not reveal which differences came from genes, environment, or measurement.

Evidence: first-party hand-drawn graph, calculations, and CER response.

Thursday: Environment, inheritance, and scientific limitations

Focus: separating observations from explanations.

Read: A gardener grows genetically similar seedlings in two settings. Group A receives adequate water and light; Group B receives much less light. After three weeks, Group A averages 17 cm and Group B averages 11 cm. This classroom scenario is hypothetical. The difference is consistent with light affecting growth, but experiment design, sample size, and other conditions determine how confidently it can be interpreted.

Analyze: (1) Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, and three conditions to keep constant. (2) Find the difference between mean heights. (3) Describe how random assignment and multiple seedlings make the investigation stronger. (4) Explain why environment can change a measured phenotype without changing the DNA sequence. (5) Draft a responsible conclusion that does not claim genetic proof.

Evidence: investigation plan, data interpretation, and revised conclusion.

Friday: Evidence-based mastery and reflection

Independent mastery tasks: (A) Define gene, allele, chromosome, and trait in context. (B) Complete a Bb × bb Punnett square for a fictional single-gene plant trait, reporting genotype proportions. (C) Compare inherited information with environmental influence using one example and one limitation. (D) Write a 5–7 sentence claim-evidence-reasoning explanation based on Wednesday's ten-plant measurements.

Teacher-reviewed rubric (20 points): A—4 points for accurate vocabulary; B—6 for model, outcomes, and interpretation; C—4 for accurate comparison and limitation; D—6 for claim, quantitative evidence, reasoning, and clarity. A minimum of 16/20 meets this performance-evidence target. Formal weekly mastery is awarded only through the Academy's verified assessment engine and adult-reviewed evidence process.

Model answers — open after completing your work

Bb × bb produces Bb, bb, Bb, bb: 50% Bb and 50% bb in this simplified model. Wednesday's sample has a range of 7 cm and a mean of 16 cm; genetic causation cannot be inferred from heights alone. Group A's hypothetical mean exceeds Group B's by 6 cm. Strong reasoning distinguishes modeled probabilities from guaranteed individual outcomes.

Remediation: If below 80%, reteach the specific concept, complete a fresh example with different alleles or sample numbers, revise the CER response, and reassess. Keep the initial and revised evidence in the portfolio.
