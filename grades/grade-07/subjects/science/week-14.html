Week 14 · Science & Engineering

Informational Text and Argument Analysis

How can readers evaluate whether an argument is strong?

Monday — Build a particle model

Learn and model: Matter is composed of particles. In a solid the particles remain near fixed positions and vibrate; in a liquid they move past one another; in a gas they move freely and fill available space. A model is a simplified explanation, not a photograph of invisible particles. Heating typically increases average particle kinetic energy. Distinguish observation from model-based inference.

Practice and evidence: Draw and label the particle arrangement of ice, liquid water, and water vapor. Write three sentences explaining what changes and what remains the same.

Tuesday — Use evidence to compare models

Learn and model: A sealed container with 100 g of water still has 100 g of matter after it freezes, assuming nothing enters or leaves. The state changes, not the amount of matter. A model claiming particles themselves expand into larger water particles is misleading; their spacing and arrangement change. Conservation of mass helps scientists test claims.

Practice and evidence: Compare two sketches: A shows the same number of particles more spread out; B shows each particle becoming three times larger. Which better models a phase change, and why?

Wednesday — Design a safe investigation

Learn and model: Use two identical covered cups of water, one in a warmer location and one cooler (never heat sealed containers). Record initial water amounts, approximate temperature, elapsed time, and observations. If no thermometer is available, compare room-temperature versus cooler water and explicitly report the missing measurement. Do not taste laboratory samples. A fair comparison changes one planned variable and keeps container type and initial volume the same.

Practice and evidence: Prepare a data table with columns for cup, starting condition, observations after 10 minutes, possible confounders, and conclusion. Explain why observations alone cannot directly reveal particle movement.

Thursday — Analyze the argument

Learn and model: Claim: 'All liquids evaporate at the same rate.' This claim is not supported by a single observation of water. Rate depends on multiple conditions, including temperature, exposed area, airflow, and properties of the liquid. Evidence must be relevant and sufficiently varied; a scientific argument should acknowledge uncertainty and suggest a test.

Practice and evidence: Write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph using your observations or the supplied hypothetical data: A loses 4 mL and B loses 1 mL over the same period. Explain at least one limitation; label these numbers as fictional.

Friday — Independent scientific defense

Learn and model: Create a one-page explanation answering: How can particle models help explain changes of state without claiming scientists see the particles directly? Include diagrams for two states, a conservation-of-matter explanation, a testable question, and one limitation of your model.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20 points): scientific accuracy 6; diagrams and explanations 5; evidence/reasoning 5; limitations and revision 4. Add work to the established portfolio; complete the normal weekly assessment separately.
