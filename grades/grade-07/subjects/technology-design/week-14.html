Week 14 · Technology, Design & Tools

Informational Text and Argument Analysis

How can readers evaluate whether an argument is strong?

Monday — Read charts as arguments

Learn and model: A chart makes a claim through its choices: title, axes, scale, categories, and labels. In this fictional library survey, 20 of 40 students prefer longer hours, 12 prefer more books, and 8 prefer more computers. The percentages are 50%, 30%, and 20%. A bar chart should show category names and a scale beginning at zero unless a different choice is clearly justified.

Practice and evidence: Draw a labeled bar chart using the supplied fictional counts. State one supported conclusion and one conclusion the survey does not support.

Tuesday — Evaluate a misleading graph

Learn and model: Suppose a graph compares 48 and 50 visitors but begins its vertical axis at 47. The small difference looks much larger than it is. The numerical increase is 2 visitors, or roughly 4.17% relative to 48. Truncated axes are not always wrong, but must be clear and suitable for the intended comparison.

Practice and evidence: Sketch a zero-based graph and a 47-based graph of the same numbers. Explain how framing could influence a reader.

Wednesday — Understand samples and uncertainty

Learn and model: Sampling determines whose preferences a dataset describes. A survey of 40 students cannot establish the views of an entire town without evidence that the sample represents that population. Missing responses, question wording, and who was invited matter. Never label fictional counts as real survey results.

Practice and evidence: Design a neutral two-question survey and a simple data collection sheet. Identify two forms of selection bias and how you would reduce them.

Thursday — Build an accessible data presentation

Learn and model: Clear data visualization includes descriptive headings, textual summaries, readable labels, adequate contrast, and non-color-only distinctions. A screen-reader user should understand the takeaway from accompanying text even without viewing the chart. Display sources, date range, and limitations when available.

Practice and evidence: Create an accessible paper or digital chart from the fictional library survey. Add an alternative-text paragraph and a methodological note saying the data are invented.

Friday — Independent verification and defense

Learn and model: A claim supported by data should survive basic arithmetic and source checks. For the library survey, the counts sum to 40 and percentages to 100%. Explain how you would detect a mislabeled denominator or a chart that conceals categories. Your final product should serve readers with different visual and technical access needs.

Practice and evidence: Submit the chart, its descriptive text, a two-sentence supported claim, and a critique of one misleading design choice. Rubric (20): numeric accuracy 6; visual clarity 4; source critique 5; accessibility 5. Retain the existing weekly assessment.
