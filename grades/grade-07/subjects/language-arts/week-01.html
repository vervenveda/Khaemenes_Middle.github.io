Week 01 · Language Arts & Literacy

Seventh Grade Scholar Launch

How do seventh-grade scholars prove independence through evidence, revision, and responsibility?

English Evidence Journey · Week 01

Investigate a fictional school-library experiment using an informational report and competing opinions. Learn to cite multiple sources, assess sampling limits, and revise a fair argument.

Multiple source evidence Point of view and rhetoric Qualified claims Counterclaim and revision

Open one day at a time. Students can use existing portfolio/printable tools to preserve work; these lesson pages do not automatically save written responses.

Monday · Read a report and inspect its evidence

Learning target: Identify the report’s supportable conclusion, numerical details, and limits.

Start reading and learning
Academy-original report: The Riverbend Library Pilot

Riverbend Middle School tested one additional hour of supervised library use on four Fridays. The building stayed open until 5:30 p.m. rather than 4:30 p.m. The extra-hour sign-in sheet recorded 41 visits on the first Friday, then 35, 38, and 45. These were visits, not distinct students; one student could sign in on all four Fridays.

Several students told staff the extra hour helped them study after school activities. A family volunteer worried that some students had no transportation after the late bus. The librarian said more sessions might be possible if enough adults were available. The report also noted building and staffing costs.

In a voluntary survey, 90 families responded: 52 supported extending the pilot, 28 opposed it, and 10 were undecided. These answers were not collected from all families; people chose whether to respond. The report recommended another monitored trial with clearer costs, transport information, and attendance data before any permanent decision.

Source: Academy-original fictional report. All numbers are invented for this reading exercise.

Worked example: Model: “Forty-five late-hour visits occurred on the fourth Friday” supports the claim that some students used the extension. It does not prove that 45 unique students improved their grades.

Student practice
  1. Identify two factual observations from different paragraphs.
  2. Calculate the number of reported extra-hour visits and explain why it cannot be called the number of unique students.
  3. Name two limits that would affect a permanent policy decision.
  4. Write one focused question the school should investigate next.

Today’s portfolio evidence: A claim, two cited details, an evidence limitation, and a research question.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Insist that conclusions match the data. A voluntary survey does not describe every family.

Tuesday · Compare two competing voices

Learning target: Analyze how different authors use experience, numbers, and rhetorical emphasis.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Academy-original paired letters: Two Voices at Riverbend

Student representative: “Late library hours gave club members a quiet place to work. The sign-ins show that students repeatedly used the hour. We should keep testing the program and learn who benefits.”

Family volunteer: “I value library access, but keeping the building open requires supervisors and safe travel home. One family in my group cannot pick up a student after the late bus. We need a realistic plan before making a permanent promise.”

Both fictional letters describe meaningful concerns. Neither is a random survey or an independent causal experiment.

Worked example: Model: The student’s main reason is opportunity for after-school study; the volunteer’s strongest concern is access and staffing. A fair comparison acknowledges both without misrepresenting either.

Student practice
  1. Identify each letter’s claim, audience, and one supporting detail.
  2. Find an example of personal experience and explain its value and limits.
  3. Which text is best for counting library visits? Which is best for hearing a transport concern? Explain.
  4. Write a two-sentence summary of the letter you initially agree with less.

Today’s portfolio evidence: An impartial comparison chart and a fair counterclaim.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Have students restate each position in terms its author would recognize; disagreement does not justify distortion.

Wednesday · Build an evidence chain from multiple texts

Learning target: Support a qualified position using cited evidence and an explicit limitation.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Three texts, one research question

Use the Riverbend report and both letters. A qualified claim recognizes what available evidence supports and what remains untested.

Worked example: Worked example: “Riverbend should run another monitored pilot before committing to permanent late hours.” Evidence: repeated sign-ins (report paragraph 1). Counterevidence: transportation and staffing may limit equitable access (volunteer letter). Reasoning: another trial can test those uncertainties.

Student practice
  1. Formulate a conditional, arguable claim about the library hours.
  2. Create two separate claim–evidence–reasoning chains from different documents.
  3. Explain why 52 of 90 voluntary survey responses do not prove all families agree.
  4. Describe the one additional fact most likely to change your position.

Today’s portfolio evidence: Two attributed evidence chains plus a limitation statement.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Check that each source contributes a distinct fact or perspective, not a duplicate statement.

Thursday · Draft an argument and respond to counterevidence

Learning target: Develop a comparative argument and revise overgeneralization and weak reasoning.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Student writing challenge

Prompt: Should the school extend Riverbend’s pilot before adopting a permanent schedule? A student can reasonably argue for another test, against it, or for a conditional option.

Worked example: Revision model: Replace “Everybody wants this change” with “Fifty-two of ninety voluntary respondents supported the extension, but that sample cannot establish the views of all school families.”

Student practice
  1. Write 190–260 words containing a claim, two cited details, and explanatory reasoning.
  2. Include the strongest credible counterclaim from the text you disagree with.
  3. Identify a vague generalization and a missing transition.
  4. Rewrite two sentences and show the old and new versions.

Today’s portfolio evidence: An original short argument, fair counterclaim, citation notes, and revision trace.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Score reasoning and quality of evidence, not whether the learner favors the proposed schedule.

Friday · Challenge an inaccurate claim and assemble evidence

Learning target: Correct a misstatement of evidence and explain what the writing revision improved.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Source-based correction

A fictional social post reads: “The Riverbend survey proves that every family demands permanent late hours.” Check that claim against the actual report.

Worked example: Model correction: the survey had 52 supportive responses among 90 voluntary responses. Because not all families responded, the survey cannot establish universal support.

Student practice
  1. Identify the exact words that overstate the evidence.
  2. Rewrite the post in two accurate sentences with a number and a sampling limitation.
  3. Revise and preserve Thursday’s draft for your portfolio.
  4. Reflect on which new source information might change your argument.

Today’s portfolio evidence: Teacher-reviewed 20-point rubric: qualified claim 4, multiple accurate evidence 6, reasoning 4, fair counterclaim 3, revision/citation 3. This is developmental evidence, not official mastery.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Accept well-supported opposing arguments. The existing verified randomized assessment controls the 80% weekly gate.

This week’s instructional practice and teacher rubrics never replace the verified 80% Academy assessment engine. Learner work remains separate from academic mastery.
