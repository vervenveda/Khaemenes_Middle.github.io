Week 14 · Language Arts & Literacy

Informational Text and Argument Analysis

How can readers evaluate whether an argument is strong?

Monday — Read an original informational argument

Learn and model: Original Academy passage: “The town library is considering opening one additional evening each week. Supporters say an evening schedule may help families who work during the day. During a two-week trial, 42 visitors entered after 5 p.m. on the first Thursday and 39 on the second. The library has not recorded how many visitors would have come at other times. Staffing an extra evening would require reallocating employee hours. Before adopting a permanent schedule, the town should compare several trial evenings, survey users who cannot currently attend, and publish the staffing cost.” The central claim recommends collecting more evidence before deciding. A claim is the position; evidence is information offered in support; reasoning explains the connection.

Practice and evidence: Annotate the recommendation, two numerical details, one limitation of the data, and one unanswered question. In 80 words explain why 42 and 39 visits alone do not prove a permanent change is justified.

Tuesday — Test source credibility and inference

Learn and model: The passage provides numbers but no sampling method, total visitor counts, or comparison days. Accurate figures may still support weak conclusions if important context is missing. Source evaluation asks who produced information, how observations were collected, what is omitted, and whether another source can confirm the conclusion. An inference is reasonable interpretation, not an explicitly observed fact.

Practice and evidence: Sort these claims as supported, uncertain, or unsupported: “Some people entered after 5 p.m.” “The trial increased total visits.” “Every local family prefers evenings.” Quote or paraphrase the exact information underlying your decision.

Wednesday — Compare competing recommendations

Learn and model: Position A: “Adopt an evening immediately because 81 visits occurred across two trial nights.” Position B: “Repeat a longer trial and compare total visits, costs, and accessibility before a permanent decision.” Neither recommendation can be judged by its confidence alone. Identify the evidence each uses, likely assumptions, and the additional data needed. A counterclaim is an alternative position answered with reasons rather than dismissed.

Practice and evidence: Create an argument map with claim, two reasons, evidence, counterclaim, and response. State one fair point for the position you ultimately reject.

Thursday — Draft, quote, and revise

Learn and model: A precise paragraph separates observation from interpretation: “The trial counted 42 and 39 evening entries, but without a comparison period those figures cannot establish a net increase in use.” A writer should attribute information to the supplied Academy library scenario rather than imply the data are real town records. Quotations preserve wording; paraphrases preserve meaning and still name the source.

Practice and evidence: Write a 180–250-word editorial recommending a next step. Cite two details from the original fictional scenario, acknowledge one limitation, and answer one counterargument. Revise unsupported absolutes such as “always” and “everyone.”

Friday — Independent argument defense

Learn and model: Prepare a two-minute oral or written defense of your recommendation. Explain the source's origin, why its small trial is informative but inconclusive, and what evidence would change your mind. Students working independently may write a transcript instead of recording audio; no external account or online source is required.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20 points): clear claim 4; accurate evidence and attribution 6; reasoning and counterclaim 6; revision and source critique 4. Preserve the final artifact in the established portfolio. Complete the existing weekly assessment independently.
