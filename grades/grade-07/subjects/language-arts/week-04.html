Week 04 · Language Arts & Literacy

Percent Change and Civic Data

How can percent reasoning support informed civic decisions?

Read · Compare · Challenge · Argue · Revise

A source-critical civic reading investigation using a fictional community reading-corner project. Compare survey percentages with percentage-point change, evaluate competing claims, and write a qualified argument that respects different voices.

Argument analysis Percent change vs points Source credibility Qualified counterargument

Expand one day at a time. Save drafts through the Academy's existing journal, printable or portfolio process; these static lessons do not automatically save essays.

Monday · Read a report and locate the argument

Learning target: Distinguish recorded civic data from conclusions about why reported preferences might have changed.

Begin with the original survey investigation
Academy-original report · The Reading Corner Follow-Up

The fictional Harbor Learning Council considered placing an evening reading corner in a community building. Before its six-week pilot, volunteers invited families to answer a voluntary question: “Would an evening reading corner be useful?” Eighty people answered, and 40 selected “yes,” or 50% of respondents. Some families had not heard about the survey, and others chose not to participate.

After the pilot, volunteers asked the same question on a new voluntary form. Ninety people replied, and 54 selected “yes,” or 60% of that group. The proportions differed by 10 percentage points. The survey did not identify whether the second set of respondents consisted of the same people as the first.

During the pilot, the building stayed open on selected evenings and kept a sign-in sheet. Staff noted that some visitors came several times, so total visits could not be interpreted as different people. Volunteers also heard concerns about transportation and supervision that were not captured by the simple yes-or-no question.

The council's report concluded that the second survey included a larger supporting share of its respondents. It did not claim that the pilot caused an increase in support across the whole community. It recommended clearer sampling, attendance records, and an accessibility review before any permanent decision.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional report, English 7 Week 04. Numbers, public bodies and surveys are invented.

Evidence model: First survey: 40/80 = 50%. Second survey: 54/90 = 60%. The change is 10 percentage points among two different voluntary response groups. It is not proof that any given person's opinion changed or that the pilot caused the difference.

Student practice
  1. Annotate the report’s purpose, two percentages and the respondent group described in each.
  2. Identify one narrow conclusion supported by paragraph 2 and one overbroad conclusion that is not supported.
  3. Explain why the two response groups cannot automatically be treated as a before-and-after study of the same people.
  4. Write a clear three-sentence summary with source and paragraph notes and one stated limitation.

Today's portfolio evidence: An annotated report, an accurate 50%-to-60% comparison, and an evidence-limited summary.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Do not conflate respondents with all residents. Ask which parts of the report are observations, which are assumptions and what further evidence would matter.

Tuesday · Explain percent change and percentage points

Learning target: Accurately distinguish two different ways of describing the same proportional difference.

Expand today's reading and practice
Academy-original explanatory note · Two Ways to Describe Change

If a response share moves from 50% to 60%, it increases by 10 percentage points. The relative percent change in the reported share is calculated using the original 50% as its base: (60 − 50) ÷ 50 = 0.20, or a 20% relative increase. The two sentences describe different measurements, even though each is mathematically meaningful.

In another fictional record, participation sign-ups rose from 40 to 50 during a different event. This is an increase of 10 sign-ups; relative to the original 40, it is a 25% increase in sign-up count. Sign-ups are not necessarily completed visits, and neither number establishes how many distinct people benefited.

The base, unit and source determine whether numerical language is honest. A statement such as “support increased by 20 points” would be incorrect for a move from 50% to 60%. A statement that “the community became 20% more supportive” may also overclaim because the two surveys had different volunteer samples.

Source: Academy-original fictional instructional note, Grade 7 Week 04; all figures are examples.

Worked distinction: The change from 50% to 60% equals +10 percentage points, or a 20% relative increase in the reported share (10 divided by 50). The count change from 40 to 50 equals +25% relative to the 40 starting sign-ups. These are not interchangeable facts.

Student practice
  1. Explain in two complete sentences why 10 percentage points and 20% relative increase are different terms.
  2. Calculate the percent change from 40 to 50 sign-ups and identify its base and unit.
  3. Correct the claim “Support rose 20 percentage points from 50% to 60%” and explain its error.
  4. Write two accurate chart captions, one for survey proportions and one for event sign-up counts, each with source context.

Today's portfolio evidence: A percentage-point versus relative-change comparison and two attributed data captions.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Keep the mathematical explanation tied to the meaning of the text. This English exercise does not award scores in the Mathematics course.

Wednesday · Evaluate competing civic arguments

Learning target: Distinguish persuasive rhetoric from evidentiary quality and represent opposing concerns fairly.

Expand today's reading and practice
Academy-original opinion · The Pilot Settled Everything

“After the reading corner opened, community support jumped by twenty points. The figures prove the pilot made everyone happier. We should approve permanent evening hours immediately, and anyone asking for another study simply does not care about books.”

Academy-original response · Keep Studying Access

“The second survey has a higher supportive share than the first, which gives us reason to consider the project. But the increase is ten percentage points, not twenty. The groups chose to answer voluntarily, and transportation remains uncertain. We can support reading access while checking who could actually use the schedule.”

These are invented opinion texts responding to the fictional report. Neither the force of a statement nor a writer's good intentions changes the underlying survey evidence.

Rhetorical analysis: The first opinion uses an incorrect numerical term (“twenty points”), an unsupported causal claim (“proves”), and an attack on dissenters. A fair response can support the reading corner while correcting all three reasoning errors.

Student practice
  1. Identify each author's claim, purpose and intended audience, citing a sentence from each opinion.
  2. Mark the first editorial's numerical error, causal overclaim and dismissive rhetoric.
  3. Build a claim–evidence–reasoning chain supporting a qualified conclusion from the original report.
  4. Write the strongest fair counterargument to your preferred position using a real concern from paragraph 3 of the report.

Today's portfolio evidence: A rhetorical analysis chart, a corrected claim and a fair counterargument grounded in two sources.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Different policy preferences can be equally reasoned. Teach students to challenge facts and logic, not characterize people as uncaring for seeking more evidence.

Thursday · Draft a comparative civic argument

Learning target: Use correctly labeled percentages, multiple sources and a counterclaim in a substantial writing product.

Expand today's reading and practice
Writing question · What should Harbor investigate next?

The fictional Harbor Learning Council wants a recommendation. You may propose a longer pilot, permanent access with safeguards, a different schedule or additional research. Justify your position through the report and both opinion texts rather than treating any view as automatically correct.

Address this evidence challenge directly: the 50%-to-60% difference compares two voluntary respondent groups, so it cannot establish that the same families changed their opinions after the pilot.

Revision model: Weak: “The pilot proved twenty percent of the city switched to favoring the reading corner.” Stronger: “Support was reported by 50% of the first voluntary sample and 60% of the second, a difference of 10 percentage points between respondent groups; the report cannot establish whether the pilot changed individual opinions (Reading Corner Follow-Up, paragraphs 1–2).”

Student practice
  1. Draft 210–290 original words with a qualified claim, two accurately cited data points and one source-based limitation.
  2. Use the phrase “percentage points” correctly and explain how it differs from relative percent change.
  3. Present one fair opposing concern about supervision, transportation or sampling and respond to it.
  4. Revise one overgeneralized phrase and one weak evidence explanation, saving the original and improved versions.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 210–290-word sourced argument, fair counterclaim, correctly described percent change and an explicit revision trace.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Score conceptual accuracy and reasoning before grammar. Students should not be required to endorse any specific policy to receive positive instructional feedback.

Friday · Challenge a misleading claim and reflect

Learning target: Independently correct a data misstatement, defend a fair conclusion and prepare portfolio evidence.

Expand today's reading and practice
Academy-original poster challenge · Did Everyone Change Their Mind?

“The reading corner convinced 20% of all Harbor families to support evening programs!”

Review the two surveys and the council report. Ask what the statement assumes about the participants, the denominator and causal influence of the pilot.

Accurate correction: The reported supporting shares differed from 50% to 60% across two voluntary response groups. This is a 10-percentage-point difference (20% relative to the first reported share), but the surveys do not establish what all Harbor families thought or what changed their opinions.

Student practice
  1. Name three unsupported assumptions in the poster, with paragraph references from the report.
  2. Rewrite the poster using exact survey proportions and no unsupported causal claim.
  3. Present a one-minute defense of your recommendation, acknowledging the best opposing evidence.
  4. File your original and revised Thursday draft, data captions and a four-sentence reflection about percentage-point language.

Today's portfolio evidence: Corrected poster, short oral defense, revised comparative argument and reflection. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: accurate claims 4, numerical/source evidence 6, reasoning 4, fair counterargument 3, revision 3. Not an official course grade.
