Week 11 · Language Arts & Literacy

Probability and Fair Games

How can probability help us judge risk, fairness, and prediction?

Monday — Read an original argument

Learn and model: Original fictional game-club notice: 'Our spinner is perfectly fair. It landed on blue 8 times in 10 spins, so blue is clearly the winning choice.' Ten observed spins cannot prove fairness or a dependable winning strategy.

Practice and evidence: Underline the notice's claim and evidence, then identify one unsupported inference.

Tuesday — Compare viewpoints

Learn and model: Original dialogue: 'Lea: Blue appeared often, so it must be more likely. Ravi: The spinner may be even, but ten spins are too few to know.' Both students interpret the same evidence differently; neither has established the device's underlying probabilities.

Practice and evidence: Write an impartial summary of the disagreement and a question that would clarify the evidence.

Wednesday — Recognize persuasive wording

Learn and model: Words such as perfectly and clearly signal certainty. A trustworthy information text distinguishes outcomes seen in a sample from the design of the spinner.

Practice and evidence: Rewrite the fictional notice as a cautious report with exact counts and an explicit limitation.

Thursday — Research without invented citations

Learn and model: A source's author, date, methods and conflicts of interest influence how readers should evaluate its claims. Never manufacture a citation: students can evaluate the provided Academy passage without pretending to have consulted a real study.

Practice and evidence: Write a comparison paragraph distinguishing anecdote, observed frequency and tested probability.

Friday — Independent editorial

Learn and model: Compose a 180–230-word response arguing whether the club should advertise its spinner as fair; present a counterclaim and propose a stronger test.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): passage evidence 6, analysis 6, counterargument 4, revision 4. Existing assessment untouched.
