Week 11 · Language Arts & Literacy

Probability and Fair Games

How can probability help us judge risk, fairness, and prediction?

Monday — Read an original argument

Learn and model: Original fictional game-club notice: 'Our spinner is perfectly fair. It landed on blue 8 times in 10 spins, so blue is clearly the winning choice.' Ten observed spins cannot prove fairness or a dependable winning strategy.

Practice and evidence: Underline the notice's claim and evidence, then identify one unsupported inference.

Week 11 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 1 — Analyze the club notice

Student investigation: Underline ‘perfectly fair’ and ‘clearly’ in the fictional club notice. Separate the observed 8 blue outcomes in 10 spins from the conclusion that blue is generally more probable.

Worked teacher check: Eight in ten is an observed frequency of 80%, not proof that the spinner's true blue probability is 80% or that the spinner is biased.

Evidence product: An annotated text with claim, evidence, inference and one qualified replacement sentence.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Tuesday — Compare viewpoints

Learn and model: Original dialogue: 'Lea: Blue appeared often, so it must be more likely. Ravi: The spinner may be even, but ten spins are too few to know.' Both students interpret the same evidence differently; neither has established the device's underlying probabilities.

Practice and evidence: Write an impartial summary of the disagreement and a question that would clarify the evidence.

Week 11 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 2 — Compare dialog positions

Student investigation: Make a two-column evidence comparison for Lea and Ravi. Explain what each speaker assumes and identify what information about sectors or more trials would be useful.

Worked teacher check: Lea extrapolates from ten trials; Ravi correctly warns about limited evidence but does not prove the spinner's fairness. The spinner design remains unknown.

Evidence product: A balanced paragraph citing both speakers and a question for a fair test.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Wednesday — Recognize persuasive wording

Learn and model: Words such as perfectly and clearly signal certainty. A trustworthy information text distinguishes outcomes seen in a sample from the design of the spinner.

Practice and evidence: Rewrite the fictional notice as a cautious report with exact counts and an explicit limitation.

Week 11 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 3 — Revise certainty markers

Student investigation: Replace ‘perfectly fair’ and ‘clearly the winning choice’ with language consistent with ten observed spins. Explain why adverbs can mislead readers.

Worked teacher check: ‘Blue appeared in eight of ten recorded spins; this small sample does not establish the spinner's long-run probabilities’ preserves the data without overclaiming.

Evidence product: A two-version notice with editing annotations on qualification and precision.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Thursday — Research without invented citations

Learn and model: A source's author, date, methods and conflicts of interest influence how readers should evaluate its claims. Never manufacture a citation: students can evaluate the provided Academy passage without pretending to have consulted a real study.

Practice and evidence: Write a comparison paragraph distinguishing anecdote, observed frequency and tested probability.

Week 11 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 4 — Evaluate source reliability

Student investigation: Build a source-check checklist covering author, time, sampling procedure, evidence, conflict of interest and whether the underlying spinner design was inspected. Apply it to the fictional notice.

Worked teacher check: The club notice reports a count but offers no sector-size evidence, sampling plan or independent testing. These omissions limit claims about fairness.

Evidence product: A completed six-item source checklist and comparison between anecdote and tested probability.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Friday — Independent editorial

Learn and model: Compose a 180–230-word response arguing whether the club should advertise its spinner as fair; present a counterclaim and propose a stronger test.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): passage evidence 6, analysis 6, counterargument 4, revision 4. Existing assessment untouched.

Week 11 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 5 — Publish an evidence-based editorial

Student investigation: Write 180–230 words taking a position on advertising fairness. Quote the source, acknowledge a competing interpretation, describe a better trial and revise after checking for unsupported certainty.

Worked teacher check: A sound editorial can argue against asserting fairness until sector geometry and repeated independent trials are examined, without claiming ten spins prove unfairness.

Evidence product: Final editorial with a counterargument, revision record and a practical test proposal.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.
