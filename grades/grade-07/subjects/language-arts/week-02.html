Monday · Find theme in a school narrative

Learning target: Distinguish the narrative’s topic from its theme and support an interpretation with two moments.

Begin the Academy-original reading
Academy-original narrative · The Routes We Share

Amir carried two maps to the student council meeting. One showed a shorter walking route beside the neighborhood recreation center; the other showed the usual longer route past the library. He thought the choice was simple until Imani pointed to the map key. The shorter route crossed an unmarked street where the students had no marked crossing.

“Your map saves time for some students,” Imani said, “but not everyone lives on that side of the school.” Amir had counted the turns and estimated the distance, but he had not asked anyone how the crossing worked at dismissal. He added a question mark beside the street and asked Imani to help him gather other students' observations.

At the next meeting, a student who used a mobility aid explained that the library route had the curb ramps they needed. Another student said the shorter route would be helpful after practices if the crossing could be improved. Amir realized his first map answered only the question he had asked: Which line is shorter? The group needed to ask which route was workable for different students.

Instead of erasing his first idea, Amir wrote two headings on a fresh page: “What we know” and “What we still need to ask.” He wanted a recommendation that could be checked by the people who would use it.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fiction, Grade 7 Week 02. Imaginary students, places and routes.

Think-aloud: The topic is how students choose a school route. A defensible theme is “A fair decision requires listening to people who experience a choice differently.” Amir’s unmarked crossing and the students’ different access needs support this interpretation.

Student practice
  1. Write the story's topic in a phrase and its theme in one complete sentence.
  2. Cite one detail from paragraph 1 and another from paragraph 3 supporting the theme.
  3. Compare what Amir knows early in the story with what he learns later, using paragraph locators.
  4. Propose a second plausible theme and identify which textual detail best supports it.

Today's portfolio evidence: Theme-versus-topic note, two cited details, and a supported alternative theme.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

A theme is a message inferred from a literary text, not a one-word topic. Students should recognize accessibility as relevant without inventing facts about an actual neighborhood.

Tuesday · Compare central ideas across informational texts

Learning target: Explain an informational text's central idea and identify what a proportional comparison does not establish.

Expand lesson, examples and student activities
Academy-original informational memo · Route Survey Numbers

A fictional school committee invited two advisory groups to describe how they travel home. Group North had 16 survey respondents, and 12 selected the existing library route as workable. Group South had 24 respondents, and 18 selected the existing library route as workable. Students were allowed to identify only one route on the form.

The report stated that 12 of 16 and 18 of 24 were the same proportion: three-quarters of each responding group. Because the groups had different numbers of respondents, comparing 18 with 12 alone could suggest the second group had stronger support even though the proportions matched.

Participation was voluntary; the report did not establish what every student preferred. Its author recommended checking walking conditions, transit schedules, curb ramps, and dismissal times before treating either route as suitable for every student. The committee also encouraged students to share experiences that a simple route checkbox could not capture.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional memo with invented survey values. Not real transportation guidance.

Worked comparison: 12/16 = 0.75 and 18/24 = 0.75. The raw numbers differ, but the proportions are equal. The central idea is that route decisions need both fair comparisons and real-world context. The survey alone cannot prove a route is safe for everyone.

Student practice
  1. State the memo's central idea and identify two supporting details from different paragraphs.
  2. Calculate or simplify both proportions and explain what a raw-count comparison hides.
  3. Identify a claim the survey supports and a broader claim it cannot support.
  4. Compare the informational memo's stated central idea with the narrative's implied theme, citing both.

Today's portfolio evidence: A central-idea evidence chart, fair proportional comparison, and two-text comparison.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Keep this a reading-and-reasoning task, not a substitute for verified Math scores. Ask what happens when a writer omits denominators.

Wednesday · Evaluate a claim against both sources

Learning target: Combine literary perspective and numerical evidence to qualify an argument.

Expand lesson, examples and student activities
Academy-original community notice · A Quick Vote?

“Eighteen students in Group South chose the library route, compared with twelve in Group North. That proves South prefers the old route more strongly. We should skip further discussion and keep the same route for everyone.”

This fictional notice makes a claim about proportions and an unsupported conclusion about universal suitability. Compare it with The Routes We Share and Route Survey Numbers before accepting or rejecting its recommendation.

Reasoning model: The notice correctly reports two counts but wrongly assumes they express different levels of support: both groups recorded 75%. It also assumes a voluntary survey and a simple map establish a workable route for every student. An accurate response can acknowledge reported support while requesting accessibility and route-condition evidence.

Student practice
  1. Underline the notice's accurate numerical statement and its misleading interpretation.
  2. Write a qualified counterclaim with at least one citation from the memo and one from the narrative.
  3. Create two evidence chains, each linking a different source to your reasoning.
  4. Identify an additional source or observation that could resolve a genuine disagreement.

Today's portfolio evidence: A two-source evidence matrix and qualified response to the fictional notice.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Separate evaluation of an author's rhetoric from personal agreement. Encourage students to specify what evidence would alter their conclusion.

Thursday · Draft a comparative argument and revise

Learning target: Explain theme and central idea, compare sources and data, and answer a counterclaim fairly.

Expand lesson, examples and student activities
Writing workshop · Evidence and the limits of a quick comparison

Writing prompt: How can evidence and fair comparison help a community make a decision that works for more people? Use the story, memo and community notice. You may recommend more investigation, a different route, or a conditional pilot, but you must justify your stance from the documents.

Revision demonstration: Weak: “More students in the South group want the library route, so it is better.” Improved: “Although 18 South-group respondents and 12 North-group respondents selected the library route, both figures represent 75% of their respective responding groups; the figures do not establish which route is workable for all students (Route Survey Numbers, paragraphs 1–3).”

Student practice
  1. Write 190–260 original words with an arguable claim, at least two cited sources, and clearly explained details.
  2. Include one discussion of the narrative's theme and the memo's central idea.
  3. Describe a fair counterargument and answer it using evidence rather than an insult or exaggeration.
  4. Rewrite one numerical overclaim and one weak transition; preserve the original and revised versions.

Today's portfolio evidence: A comparative 190–260-word argument, two citations, a fair counterclaim and visible revision trace.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

A well-reasoned different conclusion should receive full credit. Evaluate whether the writer uses numbers and literary details appropriately.

Friday · Analyze independently and reflect

Learning target: Distinguish supportable conclusions from overstatements and present a portfolio-ready revision.

Expand lesson, examples and student activities
Independent short text · An Oversimplified Headline

“South Students Favor the Old Route Twice as Much as North Students.”

The headline is invented for practice. Compare it against the stated 12-of-16 and 18-of-24 results before commenting on it.

Correction model: Both groups have the same responding share (75%). It is inaccurate to claim that one group favored the route twice as much based only on the different counts. The voluntary survey also does not represent all students.

Student practice
  1. Explain exactly why the headline is misleading using both fractions or percentages.
  2. Revise the headline to report the survey results without overstating them.
  3. Submit Thursday's first and revised drafts with a source matrix and a paragraph explaining the most important improvement.
  4. Reflect in 4 sentences on how theme, central idea, and numeric evidence can inform different parts of one argument.

Today's portfolio evidence: A corrected headline, comparative essay revisions, and reflection. Teacher-reviewed rubric /20: text interpretations 4, accurate evidence and comparisons 6, reasoning 4, fair counterclaim 3, meaningful revision 3. No automatic mastery or grade.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

The Week 02 verified assessment remains the only student-facing score gate. This writing rubric is for adult coaching and portfolio evidence.

Reading practice and human-reviewed writing are developmental. Formal assessments, previous scores, and the Academy 80% mastery requirement are not changed by this page.
