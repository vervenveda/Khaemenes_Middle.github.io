Week 03 · Language Arts & Literacy

Unit Rates, Slope Foundations, and Informational Text

How can rates reveal relationships in texts, data, and the world?

Read · Compare · Question · Defend · Revise

Interpret competing voices in Academy-original texts about a traveling book cart. Use accurate unit-rate comparisons to evaluate point of view, separate observation from persuasion, and write a qualified multi-source argument.

Point of view and purpose Unit-rate evidence Source comparison Fair counterargument

Lessons expand one day at a time. Use the existing learning journal, printable or student portfolio to preserve responses; writing is not automatically saved by this page.

Monday · Read a narrative through point of view

Learning target: Explain how a narrator's knowledge, concerns, and perspective influence what the reader understands.

Read the original narrative and begin
Academy-original narrative · A Cart Full of Questions

I thought the book-cart project would be easy to judge. Our club's cart had brought a stack of books to the community center, and I had helped record each trip. We made three trips that afternoon and delivered 18 books. When another team arrived, I saw their cart loaded with even more books. “They beat us,” I whispered to my partner. I had already decided that success meant a bigger pile at the end.

Then their coordinator, Jo, unfolded a delivery sheet. The other group had made four trips to move 20 books. Jo also explained that several books were oversized and needed extra space. I looked at my own totals again and realized I hadn't compared the number of trips. I still felt proud of our work, but the question had changed: Did a larger total always mean a more productive trip?

A visitor asked why we had selected the community center rather than the youth shelter across town. I wanted to say the center had been the obvious choice, but our team had not spoken to the other group about its needs. Our cart numbers could describe deliveries; they could not reveal whether everyone in the neighborhood had been served fairly.

I wrote three headings on the back of the sheet: “What I saw,” “What I assumed,” and “What I still need to ask.” Jo suggested comparing the groups' rates before the next volunteer meeting. For the first time, I understood that my position behind the cart shaped what I noticed.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional first-person narrative, Grade 7 Week 03. Trips, books, and places are invented.

Worked interpretation: The first-person narrator assumes that the team with more total books “wins.” That reaction exposes a limited point of view. Paragraphs 2 and 4 show the narrator learning that trips and community needs also matter.

Student practice
  1. Identify the narrator's initial assumption and quote the phrase revealing it, with a paragraph locator.
  2. Explain what the narrator knows directly versus what Jo and the visitor introduce.
  3. Write a second-person or third-person restatement of one event and describe what changes when the narrator's “I” disappears.
  4. Infer one reasonable limitation of the narrator's perspective without pretending to know the visitor's private motives.

Today's portfolio evidence: Point-of-view annotation, two exact text references, and a short comparison of narration choices.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Ask which narrator facts are observed and which judgments are conclusions. A first-person voice is not automatically unreliable, but its knowledge is limited.

Tuesday · Read a source report and compare rates

Learning target: Identify an informational text's purpose, evidence, and limitations when it compares quantities.

Expand today's reading and writing work
Academy-original informational memo · Book-Cart Delivery Comparison

Two fictional student teams used book carts for an afternoon delivery exercise. Team Cedar delivered 18 books in three trips; Team Willow delivered 20 books in four trips. In the memo, a “trip” meant one completed route between a collection room and the destination. Different trips could carry different book types and sizes.

For Team Cedar, the recorded average was 18 ÷ 3 = 6 books per trip. For Team Willow, 20 ÷ 4 = 5 books per trip. Cedar's recorded rate was larger, although Willow delivered more books in total. That contrast demonstrates why a reader must compare like quantities instead of assuming that the highest total proves the highest rate.

The memo did not track travel time, book weight, assistance needs, transport cost, or how many people received books. It warned readers not to treat books per trip as a complete measure of usefulness or fairness. The writer recommended tracking several dimensions before deciding how to organize future deliveries.

Source: Academy-original fictional informational memo, Week 03. The counts are instructional examples, not records of actual school volunteers.

Worked comparison: Willow's total (20) is larger than Cedar's (18), but Cedar's average books per trip (6) is larger than Willow's (5). Neither measure establishes which program delivered greater community benefit, because the memo omits recipients, needs, and effort.

Student practice
  1. Create a chart of each team's total books, number of trips, and average books per trip, citing the memo.
  2. State the memo's central idea in a single sentence and locate two supporting details.
  3. Explain why comparing 20 with 18 is different from comparing 5 with 6; identify the units each comparison needs.
  4. Identify two missing factors that would matter before turning this report into a recommendation.

Today's portfolio evidence: Unit-rate source chart, an accurately attributed central idea, and two method limitations.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Keep the figures and denominators intact. Mathematics supports literacy reasoning here; official Grade 7 mathematics scores remain under the separate assessment engine.

Wednesday · Evaluate competing perspectives and rhetorical choices

Learning target: Compare a narrator's experience with a persuasive article and determine which claims the memo supports.

Expand today's reading and writing work
Academy-original opinion column · The Bigger Pile Wins

“Team Willow carried 20 books while Team Cedar carried only 18, so Willow clearly has the best delivery system. Anyone suggesting that Cedar had a stronger result is ignoring the numbers. The committee should reward the highest total and stop wasting time on further comparisons.”

Academy-original response · A Different Question

“We should celebrate every book delivered, but the report's figures answer different questions. Willow delivered more books overall; Cedar moved more books per recorded trip. Neither number tells us who could reach the deliveries or which team needed more assistance. Let's collect that information before declaring a winner.”

Both opinion texts are fictional. Compare their point of view with the first-person narrative and the source memo; do not assume that a persuasive tone makes a claim accurate.

Rhetorical analysis: “Anyone suggesting … is ignoring the numbers” attacks disagreement instead of addressing the actual distinction between totals and rates. The response names both valid observations and acknowledges unknowns. Its qualified claim is better supported by the memo.

Student practice
  1. Identify each opinion writer's position, intended audience, and one rhetorical choice.
  2. Choose the sentence in the first column that most exaggerates the evidence; explain precisely why.
  3. Use one citation from the memo and one from the narrative to answer the “winner” argument without dismissing volunteer work.
  4. Describe how the first-person narrator and the impersonal memo guide readers toward different kinds of evidence.

Today's portfolio evidence: A two-column viewpoint comparison, rhetorical critique, and qualified response using two sources.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Discuss whether strong feelings can coexist with accurate analysis. Invite a fair reconstruction of the opinion columnist's concern before rebuttal.

Thursday · Draft a multi-source argument and revise

Learning target: Combine point of view with numerical evidence in an argument that responds fairly to counterclaims.

Expand today's reading and writing work
Writing investigation · What should success mean?

Prompt: How should a student committee evaluate the fictional book-cart trial before planning another delivery? You may favor a larger total, a higher rate, better access, or a balanced scorecard, but your argument must explain what each available source can and cannot establish.

The narrative provides one volunteer's limited perspective, the memo reports quantities, and the two opinions illustrate competing judgments. Their differences are part of the reading work.

Revision model: Weak: “Cedar did better in every way because six is greater than five.” Stronger: “Cedar averaged six books per trip versus Willow's five, but the report does not reveal delivery time or recipient needs; books per trip alone cannot establish which effort served the community best (Book-Cart Delivery Comparison, paragraphs 2–3).”

Student practice
  1. Draft 200–280 original words with a qualified claim and at least three citations from two different source types.
  2. Explain how point of view shapes one claim, and support your numerical comparison with correct denominators.
  3. Include the strongest opposing interpretation and acknowledge what evidence supports it.
  4. Revise one overstatement and one underexplained quotation, preserving before-and-after passages.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 200–280-word argued recommendation, cited quantitative comparison, fair counterargument, and revision evidence.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Readers can favor different measures of success while still reasoning well. Assess source integrity and how the writer justifies priorities, not the preferred winner.

Friday · Defend a qualified conclusion and reflect

Learning target: Independently assess source reliability and document the reasoning behind revisions.

Expand today's reading and writing work
Independent source challenge

A fictional student message states: “The book-cart memo proves Cedar's entire program helps more people because its rate is 6 rather than 5.”

Return to the memo and narrative. The message uses a genuine rate but claims an unmeasured outcome. A precise reader should separate the supported numerical comparison from the unsupported judgment about people helped.

Correction model: The memo reports a higher average books-per-trip value for Cedar, but it has no record of recipient counts or community impact (memo, paragraphs 2–3). This is a defensible correction even if the reader personally prefers Cedar's method.

Student practice
  1. Write an evidence-based correction to the fictional student message using exact units and a cited limitation.
  2. Prepare a one-minute explanation of how the narrator's viewpoint changes after comparing the rate.
  3. Submit Thursday's original and revised essay with your source chart and a paragraph explaining the strongest change.
  4. Reflect in four sentences on why an author should distinguish comparison, opinion, and evidence.

Today's portfolio evidence: Corrected claim, brief oral explanation, revised argument, and reflection. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: point-of-view analysis 4, accurate rate/source evidence 6, reasoning 4, fair counterargument 3, revision/attribution 3. Not an official course grade.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Keep oral coaching and writing rubrics developmental. The official Week 03 randomized assessment and Academy 80% mastery requirement remain unchanged.
