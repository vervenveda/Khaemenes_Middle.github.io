Week 07 · Language Arts & Literacy

Equations and Inequalities

How does solving an equation or inequality reveal hidden information?

Read · Define · Explain · Revise · Defend

Analyze original Puzzle Club and student-exhibition documents. Explain how grammar, phrasing and equation or inequality conditions affect meaning, then write a researched instructional argument.

Grammar and conventions Precision of inequalities Source comparisons Explanatory revision

Open one day at a time. Preserve student writing in the existing Academy journal, printable or portfolio; these static lessons do not automatically save drafts.

Monday · Read competing explanations of an equation

Learning target: Compare two written arguments and explain how sentence precision reveals valid and invalid steps.

Begin with the original Puzzle Club narrative
Academy-original narrative · The Puzzle Club's Missing Step

At the fictional Cedar Puzzle Club, an exhibit card posed a question: “A number plus five equals thirteen. What is the number?” Mira wrote x + 5 = 13, then explained: “Subtract five from both sides so the two quantities remain equal. The number is eight.” Her partner Eli submitted a shorter explanation: “Take away five from one side; eight is the answer.”

The teacher noticed that Eli's final value was correct but his sentence was misleading. If the writer subtracts from only one side of the equation, equality is not preserved. Mira's explanation named the operation, applied it to both sides and showed why the result followed.

The club added a reader-test rule: a good explanation must not depend on someone silently fixing the writer's omitted step. Even when the final number is correct, the language supporting it should accurately describe what happened.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional reading, Grade 7 Week 07.

Worked example: Mira's description matches x + 5 = 13 → x = 8 because both sides have five subtracted. Eli's “one side” is inaccurate. The English task evaluates the clarity and logic of the explanation.

Student practice
  1. Identify the claim, evidence and explanation in Mira's version, using paragraph references.
  2. Quote the sentence that makes Eli's explanation incomplete and correct its wording.
  3. Explain the meaning of equality and equivalent equation with a context-specific example.
  4. Write a two-sentence comparison of the writers' reasoning using a precise transition.

Today's portfolio evidence: Annotated comparison of two source explanations and a corrected argument.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

A correct numerical answer does not automatically make its accompanying explanation accurate.

Tuesday · Define the language of inequalities

Learning target: Distinguish mathematically and grammatically different claims, including boundary language.

Expand reading, example and student practice
Academy-original instructional memo · At Least Is Not More Than

A fictional student exhibition had room for a minimum of four drawings on one display board. Its planning memo stated: “The board must show at least four drawings.” In symbolic language, this means d ≥ 4, where d is the number of drawings. An editor changed the poster to “The board must show more than four drawings,” which means d > 4. The rewritten sentence would exclude a board with exactly four drawings even though the memo permits it.

A second example said that an activity space holds no more than eight chairs. This can be expressed as c ≤ 8, a maximum rather than a minimum. In everyday reading, at most, less than, at least, and more than signal different boundary conditions.

Precise grammar matters: a qualifier such as “at least” can change who or what qualifies. A writer should retain that condition when paraphrasing a document, even if the resulting sentence sounds less dramatic.

Source: Academy-original fictional instructional memo about constraint language.

Worked example: “At least four drawings” includes four; “more than four” excludes it. Both sentences are grammatically possible, but they express different constraints. The source document determines which is a faithful paraphrase.

Student practice
  1. Define variable, equation, inequality, solution and equivalent in original sentences.
  2. Write two accurate statements for d ≥ 4 and d > 4; explain which includes d = 4.
  3. Translate “no more than eight chairs” and “fewer than eight chairs” into distinct descriptions.
  4. Revise a fictional flyer that changes “at least four” to “more than four” without source support.

Today's portfolio evidence: Five-term contextual glossary, boundary comparison and corrected flyer.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Integrate punctuation and sentence completeness. The skill is how a small wording change alters the meaning.

Wednesday · Organize a process explanation and check conventions

Learning target: Describe sequential reasoning with grammatical parallelism, precise transitions and source evidence.

Expand reading, example and student practice
Academy-original editor's draft · Solving Without Skipping

The club's next card contained 2x + 3 = 11. One draft said, “First, x equal four. Next subtract something. Finally two times four plus three makes eleven.” The sentences mix fragments with unexplained changes. The reader does not see how the unknown value was determined.

An improved explanation reads: “First subtract three from each side to obtain 2x = 8. Next divide both sides by two to obtain x = 4. Finally substitute four into the original equation: 2(4) + 3 = 11.” The sequence uses parallel verbs and an observable check.

The club compared the two drafts. The revision did not merely correct punctuation; it restored a logically complete description. A source-based writer can explain mathematical evidence without pretending the numerical result alone is a sufficient argument.

Source: Academy-original procedural exposition, Grade 7 Week 07.

Worked example: A sentence fragment such as “x equal four” requires an accurate grammatical statement, for example “The variable x equals four.” “Subtract,” “divide,” and “substitute” provide parallel action verbs across consecutive steps.

Student practice
  1. Underline the fragments or ambiguous phrases in the first draft and correct them as complete sentences.
  2. Organize the revised process into three numbered steps with accurate action verbs and punctuation.
  3. Explain why the substitution check supports the conclusion x = 4.
  4. Write a 100–140-word explanation of how sentence-level revision improved the reasoning.

Today's portfolio evidence: Edited explanatory sequence, verification statement and grammar-aware comparison.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Prioritize clear subject–verb relationships and precise referents. Grammar instruction should occur within meaningful informational writing.

Thursday · Compose a nuanced evidence-based explanation

Learning target: Write an original informational argument with accurate quotation, conditional wording and complete sentences.

Expand reading, example and student practice
Academy-original writing brief · Precision Is Part of the Evidence

The fictional Puzzle Club requests a guide for younger students explaining why a solution must match every part of an original statement. Use the equation example and the inequality memo. Describe how a careless change to a word, sign or sentence can create a different claim.

Equations and inequalities express different relationships; a good composition introduces each with appropriate vocabulary, shows how to check a proposed value, and avoids claiming that all constraints behave identically.

Worked example: Weak: “If the answer is four, anything over four works too.” Stronger: “If the memo requires at least four drawings, four satisfies the condition; the phrase ‘more than four’ requires a number greater than four and excludes exactly four.” The improved statement uses exact boundaries and a grammatical contrast.

Student practice
  1. Draft a 220–300-word evidence-based guide that cites both the puzzle narrative and the exhibition memo.
  2. Explain one equation-solving operation and two distinct inequality boundary phrases.
  3. Include a fair counterpoint explaining why a quick informal explanation may seem appealing, then identify its limitation.
  4. Edit two sentence fragments, one ambiguous pronoun and one unsupported generalization; preserve before/after excerpts.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 220–300-word informational essay with source notes, counterpoint and convention-focused revisions.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Respect alternative explanation styles if logically complete and grammatical. No essay feedback modifies the official grade.

Friday · Publish a precise explanation and defend revisions

Learning target: Evaluate a misleading summary and show source-faithful grammar and evidence.

Expand reading, example and student practice
Academy-original challenge · The Poster That Changed the Rules

“Four drawings don't qualify because the sign says at least four, and x + 5 = 13 works only if you subtract five from the left side.”

The invented poster misreads the inclusive phrase “at least” and omits the need to apply the same operation to both sides of an equation. Correct the reasoning and revise the language faithfully.

Worked example: Corrected caption: “A display with exactly four drawings meets a minimum of four. For x + 5 = 13, subtract five from both sides to keep equality and obtain x = 8.”

Student practice
  1. Identify two distinct errors in the fictional poster and support each correction from the assigned texts.
  2. Write a four-sentence published caption using precise boundary terms and complete clauses.
  3. Submit Thursday's original and revised guide, glossary and evidence corrections.
  4. Reflect in four sentences on how grammar, source fidelity and mathematical language work together.

Today's portfolio evidence: Corrected display caption, revised informational guide and reflection. Educator coaching rubric /20: factual language 6, source evidence 5, structure 4, grammar/conventions 3, revision 2. Not an official grade.
