Week 24 · Social Studies, Civics & History

Reconstruction, Citizenship, and Contested Change

How did Reconstruction reshape citizenship, rights, and political power after the Civil War?

Monday — Establish the chronology

Learn and model: The U.S. Civil War ended in 1865. The 13th Amendment abolished slavery except as punishment for a crime after conviction; the 14th Amendment established birthright citizenship and equal protection; the 15th Amendment prohibited denying voting rights on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. Ratifications occurred in 1865, 1868, and 1870, respectively. Legal changes did not immediately produce equal lived experiences.

Practice and evidence: Create a dated timeline for the three amendments. Paraphrase what each addressed without claiming that it alone guaranteed enforcement.

Tuesday — Read a primary-source excerpt

Learn and model: The 14th Amendment states: 'No State shall ... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.' This is a short excerpt from a real historical document, not a complete account of the amendment. Read 'equal protection' as a constitutional principle and ask how government actions might be assessed under it.

Practice and evidence: Explain the excerpt in your own words. Distinguish what it states from one question about how the principle was applied in Reconstruction.

Wednesday — Compare law and practice

Learn and model: Reconstruction involved federal policies, new political participation by formerly enslaved people, Black elected officials, schools and community institutions, as well as violent opposition and restrictive state and local practices. The Freedmen's Bureau provided various forms of assistance, though its resources and authority were limited. Evidence should be specific about time, region, and actors rather than treating the period as a single uniform experience.

Practice and evidence: Prepare a two-column chart: 'change promised or achieved' and 'barrier or contested outcome.' Include three paired examples and distinguish policy from observed outcomes.

Thursday — Historical argument and source evaluation

Learn and model: A constitutional text reveals legal language; it cannot by itself describe daily life. To investigate implementation, historians compare laws with contemporary testimony, records, newspapers, and scholarship, considering each source's perspective and limitations. Avoid presenting invented quotations as genuine primary sources.

Practice and evidence: Write 200–250 words responding to: 'Did constitutional change alone secure equal citizenship?' Reference the amendments and at least two concrete historical circumstances; identify one source you would seek for further verification.

Friday — Independent evidence defense

Learn and model: Make a one-page Reconstruction exhibit with a timeline, concise definitions of citizenship and equal protection, and a claim about the gap between constitutional guarantees and enforcement. Include a source note for the amendment excerpt: U.S. Constitution, Amendment XIV, Section 1.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): chronological accuracy 5; constitutional understanding 5; reasoning and nuance 6; source attribution 4. Archive work in the existing portfolio and use the unchanged weekly assessment.
