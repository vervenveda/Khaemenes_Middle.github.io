Week 03 · Language Arts & Literacy

Equations, Functions, and Informational Text

How can rules and structures describe relationships in texts and mathematics?

Read · Model · Question · Synthesize · Defend

Monday · Read a narrative and examine point of view

Learning target: Infer a theme and explain what a first-person narrator knows, assumes, and must verify.

Begin the fictional study-room case
Academy-original narrative · A Room for Everyone?

When the student council asked me to recommend a study-room plan, I thought the numbers would decide everything. Our community learning center was considering two ways to pay for evening room access. Plan A charged a fixed six-credit setup fee plus two credits for each session booked. Plan B charged four credits for each session and no fixed fee. I liked Plan A because its lower per-session price sounded fair to students who used the room often.

I made a chart before asking how many sessions our group could actually attend. Omar, who came only once after sports practice, looked at my first row and frowned. “Your recommendation saves money for your schedule,” he said. “It may cost more for mine.” I had assumed our group shared the same routine because we attended the same school.

A volunteer asked whether a credit represented real money and whether booking limits had already been agreed upon. The proposal was only a classroom simulation, she reminded us; no contract or actual fees existed. We needed to label the assumptions and ask whom each plan might benefit. I still believed a consistent rule could be useful, but I no longer believed the rule could answer every question about access.

At the next council meeting, I titled my report “Costs Under the Stated Assumptions.” I presented both plans and invited the group to compare one-time users with regular visitors. My original view had not been dishonest, but it had been narrower than I realized.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fiction, English 8 Week 03. All plans, credits, and institutions are imaginary and are not a real purchase offer.

Point-of-view model: The first-person narrator first assumes that the lower per-session fee makes Plan A better. Omar's one-session situation reveals a limitation. The narration lets readers see the speaker's revision; it does not grant access to Omar's thoughts beyond what he says.

Student practice
  1. Write one plausible theme about how assumptions shape choices, and cite paragraphs 1 and 4.
  2. Identify two distinct limits of the narrator's first-person point of view with precise text locators.
  3. Explain why the narrator's initial preference is understandable but not automatically correct for all users.
  4. Rewrite one key moment from Omar's perspective without inventing private facts the story does not provide.

Today's portfolio evidence: A supported theme, narrator-perspective analysis, and carefully limited alternative viewpoint.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Keep the scenario hypothetical. Encourage the student to distinguish empathetic inference from invented knowledge and to avoid calling a narrator dishonest simply because they revise a judgment.

Tuesday · Decode a function rule in an informational report

Learning target: Translate a symbolic rule into accurate prose with its assumptions, table values, and limitations.

Expand today's source and writing workshop
Academy-original informational source · Two Study-Room Models

The fictional council summarized the options using the number of sessions, n. Plan A had a six-credit setup fee plus two credits per session: A(n) = 6 + 2n. Plan B had no setup fee and cost four credits per session: B(n) = 4n. Here, n is a nonnegative whole number of booked sessions, and the output is the number of simulated credits required.

The source tested four cases. At n = 1 session, Plan A required 8 credits and Plan B 4. At n = 2, the figures were 10 and 8. At n = 3, both required 12. At n = 4, Plan A required 14 and Plan B 16. The relationship changed as the number of sessions increased, so comparing only the fee “per session” without the setup fee would misrepresent the rule.

Neither plan included transport, accessibility, scheduling limits, or differences in who could use the room. The memo did not claim either option was universally fair or that the simulation matched a real service. Its stated purpose was to demonstrate why a rule's inputs and starting conditions matter when interpreting a claim.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original simulated informational report, Grade 8 Week 03. Credits are invented units, not currency or real fees.

Worked explanation: “Plan A begins with six credits before sessions are counted, then adds two credits for each session.” For one session A(1)=8 and B(1)=4; for three sessions A(3)=B(3)=12. The formula describes modeled credit totals, not real-world access or fairness.

Student practice
  1. Define input, output, fixed amount, per-session change, and assumption using the memo's own context.
  2. Make a four-row comparison chart for n = 1, 2, 3, 4 and verify both rules numerically.
  3. Explain in two complete sentences what the point n = 3 means and what it does not mean.
  4. Identify two relevant factors missing from the function models and explain why a fair policy would need them.

Today's portfolio evidence: A properly labeled function comparison table, a precise written rule explanation, and two stated limitations.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

This is language arts source interpretation using a simple model. Formal Grade 8 equations and functions assessment remains in Mathematics; precision and qualification are the literacy targets here.

Wednesday · Compare editorials and synthesize source credibility

Learning target: Evaluate how rhetorical choices, correct numerical statements, and missing context shape opposing arguments.

Expand today's source and writing workshop
Academy-original editorial · The Cheapest Plan Is Obvious

“Plan A is unquestionably the cheapest because it charges just two credits per session instead of four. Anyone supporting Plan B must be ignoring the rules. The school should approve A for every student without more discussion.”

Academy-original response · Ask How Many Sessions

“The lower per-session change in A is important, but it comes with an initial six-credit fee. A one-session visitor would need eight credits under A and four under B. The plans tie at three sessions. We need usage and access information before recommending a single policy for everyone.”

These are fictional opinions. A persuasive text may state one true number while omitting a decisive condition; a strong reader checks the full source before accepting the conclusion.

Evidence evaluation: Editorial 1 correctly identifies that A adds two credits per session versus B's four, but ignores A's fixed fee and overgeneralizes. Editorial 2 accurately describes a one-session case and the tie at three, while still recognizing unknown access conditions.

Student practice
  1. Annotate each editorial's claim, rhetorical technique, audience, and supporting evidence.
  2. Show exactly why the first editorial's “cheapest for everyone” claim is false using n = 1 and n = 4.
  3. Build a three-source matrix comparing the story, informational memo, and two editorials; include purpose and limitation.
  4. Write a two-sentence synthesis explaining when each modeled plan costs less and why cost alone may not decide access.

Today's portfolio evidence: Rhetorical analysis, three-source source matrix, and a qualified synthesis supported by two model cases.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Students should be free to prefer either plan under stated conditions. Evaluate the evidence and logic, not whether a learner agrees with a preferred policy outcome.

Thursday · Draft and revise a high-school-ready argument

Learning target: Support a qualified thesis with multiple sources, address counterargument, and distinguish conditions from universal claims.

Expand today's source and writing workshop
Argument prompt · What should the student council recommend?

Write for an audience of students and adult advisers. Decide whether a single plan, choice between both plans, or a longer information-gathering period is best justified by these fictional documents. The question is about reasoning from sources, not choosing a real commercial service.

Use at least three attributed details from the narrative, informational memo, and editorial viewpoints. Explain the rule and account for one-time versus frequent users.

Revision demonstration: Weak: “Plan A is always cheaper because two is less than four.” Stronger: “Although Plan A increases by two simulated credits per session, its initial six-credit fee makes it costlier for one or two sessions; the models tie at three and Plan A is lower for four, assuming the fictional fee rules are complete (Two Study-Room Models, paragraphs 1–2).”

Student practice
  1. Draft 270–370 original words with a qualified thesis, three source-specific citations, and accurate interpretation of at least two n-values.
  2. Describe the narrator's initial perspective and its revision, explaining how that matters to your argument.
  3. Represent the strongest opposing recommendation fairly and answer it without exaggerating certainty.
  4. Revise one numerical overclaim, one unsupported assumption, and one unclear transition; preserve before-and-after excerpts.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 270–370-word supported argument, source matrix, fair counterclaim, and documented substantive revision.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

High-school readiness means making scope and exceptions explicit, not simply writing more words. Assess source reasoning and fair representation before grammar polish.

Friday · Defend the rule, its limits, and your conclusion

Learning target: Respond to evidence-based challenge questions while preparing a reflective English 9 bridge artifact.

Expand today's source and writing workshop
Independent defense challenge

Reviewer A: “If Plan A adds only two credits per session, why wouldn't every student pick it?”

Reviewer B: “The rules tie at three sessions, so doesn't that prove both plans are equally fair?”

Use the fictional rules and the three kinds of sources to support your answers. A numerical tie for one input is not proof of identical cost for other inputs or equal suitability for everyone.

Defense model: Under the simulated assumptions, Plan B is less costly for one or two sessions, both tie at three, and Plan A is less costly at four. Different usage patterns and unmeasured access needs make a universal fairness claim unwarranted.

Student practice
  1. Prepare a 90-second thesis defense using n = 1, n = 3, and n = 4 with correct units.
  2. Answer both reviewer questions, citing the memo and one narrative or opinion source.
  3. Submit an original and revised analytical essay, comparison table, and three-source matrix.
  4. Write a 4–5 sentence reflection about point of view, model assumptions, and evidence habits you will use in English 9.

Today's portfolio evidence: English 9 readiness packet and brief oral defense. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: qualified thesis 4, correct multi-source evidence 6, analysis and model limitations 4, counterargument/defense 3, revision/attribution 3. Not an official grade or placement approval.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

The simulation cannot establish real financial fairness, family access, or school policy. Leave assessment and high-school placement to verified Academy processes and adult reviewers.

Source analysis, argument, and oral defense are developmental evidence, not automatic grades or mastery. Verified Academy assessments, the official 80% gate, and adult review control certification and placement.
