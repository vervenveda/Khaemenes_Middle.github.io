Week 15 · Science & Engineering

Research Writing and High School Source Skills

How do researchers synthesize multiple sources into a trustworthy explanation?

Monday — What counts as evolutionary evidence?

Learn and model: Evolution is change in inherited traits in populations across generations. Fossils document organisms from different periods; comparative anatomy identifies shared structures; DNA provides molecular evidence of ancestry. A single fossil cannot establish every step in a lineage. Scientists combine independent evidence and test explanations against observations.

Practice and evidence: Construct an evidence table with three columns: evidence type, what it can indicate, and a limitation. Explain why individual organisms do not evolve genetically during their own lifetimes.

Tuesday — Analyze an original data set

Learn and model: Hypothetical classroom dataset, not actual fossil measurements: Specimen A (older layer) has a forelimb bone length of 8 cm; B (middle layer) 10 cm; C (younger layer) 12 cm. The sequence suggests a pattern in this selected sample, but it does not prove a direct ancestor-descendant line or identify the cause of change. Relative rock-layer order is not the same as an absolute age in years.

Practice and evidence: Calculate changes A→B and B→C and total percent change from A to C. Describe two alternative explanations for the pattern and what additional data would help distinguish them.

Wednesday — Compare competing scientific explanations

Learn and model: Homologous structures have a common evolutionary origin even when their functions differ, as with forelimb bones of mammals. Similar function alone does not prove close ancestry; wings of insects and birds evolved from very different structures. A well-supported conclusion also considers geological context, traits across several species, and genetic relationships when available.

Practice and evidence: Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph comparing homology and similarity of function. Draw simple labeled forelimb diagrams; do not suggest that one modern species must have descended directly from another modern species.

Thursday — Evaluate sources and uncertainty

Learn and model: A trustworthy research explanation identifies the claim, reports methods and samples, distinguishes original research from summaries, and acknowledges uncertainty. A public infographic with no source cannot establish a fossil date. An accessible museum article may summarize peer-reviewed findings but should cite its basis. Offline students can practice source evaluation with these two fictional descriptions without external websites.

Practice and evidence: Evaluate Source A: 'Museum catalog, named collection, layer locations and referenced study'; Source B: 'Unattributed viral picture claiming proof from one fossil.' Which gives more verifiable information? Name one strength and one remaining limitation of A.

Friday — Independent research synthesis

Learn and model: Synthesize fossil order, comparative anatomy, and genetic evidence into an explanation of common ancestry. Use the classroom dataset accurately as an invented example, and do not claim it represents a real evolutionary sequence. Provide one counterinterpretation that your evidence cannot yet rule out and propose a useful additional observation.

Practice and evidence: Write 200–300 words with a claim, three types of evidence, a limitation, and a source-method note. Rubric (20 points): scientific accuracy 6; evidence distinctions 5; reasoning 5; clarity and limitations 4. File work in the established portfolio; complete the separate existing weekly assessment.
