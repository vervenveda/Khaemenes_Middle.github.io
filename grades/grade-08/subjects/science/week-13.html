Week 13 · Science & Engineering

Literary Theme, Perspective, and Modern Connections

How do authors transform old patterns into new meanings?

Monday — Map interacting Earth systems

Learn and model: Earth's atmosphere, hydrosphere, geosphere and biosphere exchange matter and energy. Evaporation transfers water to air; precipitation returns water to land and oceans; infiltration moves water through soil. Systems models simplify these interactions and should identify boundaries.

Practice and evidence: Draw a labeled water-cycle diagram with at least four transfers and one human influence. Differentiate an observed process from a model assumption.

Tuesday — Interpret a fictional climate record

Learn and model: Hypothetical classroom data, not measurements from an actual region: annual mean temperatures for years 1–5 are 18.0, 18.2, 18.1, 18.5, and 18.7°C. The first-to-last difference is 0.7°C, but five selected points do not establish a region's long-term climate trend. Weather describes short-term conditions; climate describes patterns across longer intervals.

Practice and evidence: Compute the five-value mean and the first-to-last difference. Explain why a longer, representative record is needed and why this fictional series cannot support a real-world local claim.

Wednesday — Model seasons and Earth-space geometry

Learn and model: Earth's axial tilt, approximately 23.4°, changes the angle and duration of sunlight across the year as Earth orbits the Sun. Seasons are not caused primarily by Earth's varying distance from the Sun. A hemisphere tilted toward the Sun generally experiences longer days and more direct sunlight.

Practice and evidence: Sketch Earth at two opposite orbital positions, keeping the axis parallel in the model. Label a limitation of a two-dimensional sketch and explain why opposite hemispheres have opposite seasons.

Thursday — Compare competing explanations

Learn and model: Claim A: 'Summer occurs because Earth is closest to the Sun.' Claim B: 'Seasonal sunlight patterns are chiefly related to axial tilt.' Claim B better explains opposite seasons in the two hemispheres. A sound scientific argument tests predictions and considers observations rather than relying on a familiar story.

Practice and evidence: Build a claim–evidence–reasoning response and propose an observation involving daylight length that distinguishes the explanations. Explain how a model can be corrected.

Friday — Independent Earth systems defense

Learn and model: Design a one-page explanation of how water movement and solar energy influence Earth systems. Include a correctly labeled seasonal diagram, the fictional temperature dataset with its limitations, and one question requiring additional research.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): scientific accuracy 7, quantitative reasoning 4, model quality 5, evidence limitations 4. File the artifact through existing portfolio practice; the normal weekly assessment remains separate.
