Week 12 · Science & Engineering

Probability, Statistics, and Risk

How can probability and statistics help us judge uncertainty?

Monday — Measurements and variation

Learn and model: Repeated measurements differ because of natural variation and measurement limitations. Fictional lengths in cm: 10.1, 10.3, 10.2, 10.4, 10.0. These are invented observations, not experimental results.

Practice and evidence: Compute the mean (10.2 cm) and range (0.4 cm), showing units.

Week 12 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 1 — Quantify repeated measurements

Student investigation: For fictional centimeter values 10.1, 10.3, 10.2, 10.4, 10.0, compute mean and range and write a claim about spread, with units.

Worked teacher check: The sum is 51.0 cm, so mean is 10.2 cm; range is 10.4−10.0 = 0.4 cm. Both are descriptive statistics, not estimates of instrument accuracy.

Evidence product: Labeled calculation table and a two-sentence evidence statement.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Tuesday — Estimate uncertainty

Learn and model: A mean summarizes the center, but it does not capture the whole distribution. Repeating an observation and documenting method helps identify variability. Precision of a ruler can affect reported digits.

Practice and evidence: Make a dot plot for the five fictional lengths; explain the difference between a measurement and its rounded value.

Week 12 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 2 — Distinguish precision from accuracy

Student investigation: Plot the five fictional length measurements. Explain why measurements reported to tenths of a centimeter do not demonstrate that the instrument is accurate to a tenth.

Worked teacher check: A scale marked only in centimeters may not justify tenths without a stated estimation convention; a repeatable value can still be systematically biased.

Evidence product: Dot plot, instrument-resolution note and a comparison between random variability and systematic error.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Wednesday — Probability and risk

Learn and model: In an invented classroom trial, an event occurred 6 times in 20 observations, giving an observed proportion 0.30. This is an estimate from a small sample, not a guarantee for the next trial.

Practice and evidence: Compare 6/20 with 60/200; explain why a larger sample can still have limitations.

Week 12 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 3 — Estimate event frequency and uncertainty

Student investigation: For 6 events in 20 trials and 60 events in 200 trials, convert each to a proportion; discuss what increasing trial count does not guarantee.

Worked teacher check: Both observed proportions equal 0.30, but the larger dataset may offer a more stable estimate only with suitable sampling and independence. Neither establishes the exact underlying probability.

Evidence product: Two-row fraction-decimal-percentage table and a caution about sample design.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Thursday — Evaluate an overconfident claim

Learn and model: A fictional headline states 'This result proves the event always occurs 30% of the time.' Scientific conclusions require sampling context, uncertainty, repeatability and transparent methods.

Practice and evidence: Rewrite the headline carefully and identify two missing methodological details.

Week 12 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 4 — Critique a certainty claim

Student investigation: Rewrite the fictional headline ‘This proves the event always occurs 30% of the time.’ List at least two details missing from the report and propose a replication plan.

Worked teacher check: ‘The event occurred in 6 of 20 reported trials’ describes the observations. The procedure, independent repetition and selection criteria are unspecified.

Evidence product: Revised headline, a methodological checklist and an independent-trial proposal.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Friday — Independent evidence report

Learn and model: Produce a table, summary calculations, a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph and a limitation statement for the fictional measurements and trial.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): accurate calculations 6, evidence use 5, uncertainty 5, communication 4; retain existing weekly assessment.

Week 12 Evidence Crosswalk · Day 5 — Prepare a transparent risk report

Student investigation: Create one-page report combining the length data and trial proportions with a claim, chart, calculations, uncertainty statement and real-versus-simulated label.

Worked teacher check: A justified report distinguishes invented classroom datasets from collected measurements, provides denominators and units, and states limits without asserting certainty.

Evidence product: Completed evidence report, peer audit of calculations and one explicit limitation for each dataset.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.
