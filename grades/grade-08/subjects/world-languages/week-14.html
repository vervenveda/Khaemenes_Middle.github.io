Week 14 · World Languages & Global Culture

Argument Analysis and Media Literacy

How can we evaluate whether an argument deserves our trust?

Monday — Interpretation is not word substitution

Learn and model: A literal translation can miss context and politeness. English 'Could you help me?' signals a courteous request, not uncertainty about physical ability. Formal address differs across languages.

Practice and evidence: Compare a literal and contextual translation in a language you study; label uncertain expressions.

Tuesday — Check author and audience

Learn and model: An unattributed translated quotation may omit original context. Establish who said it, when, in which language, and who translated it. Do not invent foreign-language quotations as authentic sources.

Practice and evidence: Create a source card for a clearly fictional bilingual announcement, marking its intended audience.

Wednesday — Analyze an original bilingual example

Learn and model: Fictional Spanish notice: 'La biblioteca abre a las ocho.' It means 'The library opens at eight.' 'Abre' indicates opening, not closing; missing a time zone or day remains a genuine information gap.

Practice and evidence: Translate the sentence, identify the verb, and compose a careful English announcement noting what is unknown.

Thursday — Respect cultural variation

Learn and model: A greeting or gesture may have multiple uses within one linguistic community. Avoid describing an entire culture as uniform; ask which setting and relationship apply.

Practice and evidence: Draft two polite versions of a request, with audience and formality notes; verify unfamiliar expressions in your available learning materials.

Friday — Communication defense

Learn and model: Build a bilingual mini media-literacy guide separating original text, interpretation, uncertain terms and source context.

Practice and evidence: Submit a six-line bilingual exchange and commentary. Rubric (20): meaning 7, context 5, source transparency 4, revision 4.
