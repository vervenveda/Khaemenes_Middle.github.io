Week 11 · Mathematics: Algebra I Readiness

Bivariate Data and Linear Models

How can data suggest relationships without proving causation?

Monday — Plot relationships

Learn and model: A bivariate dataset pairs two measurements. Fictional (hours studied, practice items completed) pairs: (1,4), (2,7), (3,10), (4,13). The second variable increases by three for every one-unit increase in the first; this is a deliberately constructed classroom pattern.

Practice and evidence: Plot the four pairs with axes and units; write y=3x+1 and verify every point.

Focus: bivariate data, scatterplots and linear models

Week 11 Mathematical Depth Lab · Day 1 — Graph and verify a constructed relationship

Student investigation: From (1,4), (2,7), (3,10), (4,13), calculate successive changes in y: 3, 3, 3. Label x as hours and y as items, plot the four points and explain why the plotted graph is a scatterplot rather than a connected time series.

Worked teacher check: Model y = 3x + 1. Substituting x = 1, 2, 3, 4 yields 4, 7, 10, 13; each point lies on the model.

Evidence product: A labeled scatterplot, table of successive differences and one paragraph stating a defensible association.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Tuesday — Interpret slope and intercept

Learn and model: For y=3x+1, slope 3 represents three additional items per study hour in this invented model; intercept 1 is the prediction at zero hours, not proof that one item would actually be completed.

Practice and evidence: Compute predictions at x=5 and x=0. Explain why prediction outside observed values carries uncertainty.

Focus: bivariate data, scatterplots and linear models

Week 11 Mathematical Depth Lab · Day 2 — Interpret slope and intercept responsibly

Student investigation: Compute model predictions at x = 0, x = 5 and x = 10. State which x values are observed and which require extrapolation; explain slope and intercept with units.

Worked teacher check: The predictions are 1, 16 and 31 items respectively. Slope 3 means 3 predicted items per extra study hour, but x = 10 is beyond the observed interval 1–4, so the prediction is less well supported.

Evidence product: A three-row prediction table and a 90-word cautionary interpretation.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Wednesday — Compare model and measurement

Learn and model: Fictional observations (1,4),(2,8),(3,9),(4,14) are not perfectly linear. The model y=3x+1 predicts 4,7,10,13, so residuals observed minus predicted are 0,1,−1,1.

Practice and evidence: Build a predicted/observed/residual table and explain why residuals matter.

Focus: bivariate data, scatterplots and linear models

Week 11 Mathematical Depth Lab · Day 3 — Calculate residuals and choose evidence

Student investigation: Using observations (1,4), (2,8), (3,9), (4,14) and model y = 3x + 1, make columns for predicted values, observations and observed minus predicted.

Worked teacher check: Predictions 4, 7, 10, 13 yield residuals 0, +1, −1, +1. They show deviation from an imperfect model, not an arithmetic error.

Evidence product: A complete residual table, a graphical comparison and one interpretation of positive versus negative residual.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Thursday — Challenge causal claims

Learn and model: A correlation does not establish causation: students who practice more might differ in experience or instruction. A scatterplot displays association, not the source of the pattern.

Practice and evidence: List two plausible confounders and identify which evidence would strengthen a causal claim.

Focus: bivariate data, scatterplots and linear models

Week 11 Mathematical Depth Lab · Day 4 — Challenge causality with alternatives

Student investigation: Assess the claim: ‘Studying one extra hour causes exactly three more items to be finished.’ Distinguish the constructed linear pattern from evidence about real students; propose two possible confounding variables and a better comparison.

Worked teacher check: Prior experience and access to coaching could both change item counts. An observational scatterplot alone cannot isolate the causal effect of extra study time.

Evidence product: A claim-evidence-reasoning critique and a study design indicating what to hold constant.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.

Friday — Independent modeling

Learn and model: Use fictional points (0,2),(1,4),(2,6),(3,8) to graph a relationship, build a rule and evaluate how well it fits. Distinguish an exact constructed pattern from real observations.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): correct graph 5, rule 6, verification 5, evidence limitations 4. Keep official assessment unchanged.

Focus: bivariate data, scatterplots and linear models

Week 11 Mathematical Depth Lab · Day 5 — Build and defend an independent model

Student investigation: Graph (0,2), (1,4), (2,6), (3,8), derive a linear rule, check all four pairs and discuss one reason a real dataset could disagree.

Worked teacher check: Successive differences are +2; y = 2x + 2 fits the constructed data, yielding 8 at x = 3. A perfect fit in invented values does not guarantee real-world predictive power.

Evidence product: A labeled graph, equation derivation, verification table and evidence limitations paragraph.

This is formative instructional evidence. The existing weekly assessment and learner grades remain unchanged.
