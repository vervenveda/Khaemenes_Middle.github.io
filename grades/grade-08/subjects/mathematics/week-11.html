Week 11 · Mathematics: Algebra I Readiness

Bivariate Data and Linear Models

How can data suggest relationships without proving causation?

Monday — Plot relationships

Learn and model: A bivariate dataset pairs two measurements. Fictional (hours studied, practice items completed) pairs: (1,4), (2,7), (3,10), (4,13). The second variable increases by three for every one-unit increase in the first; this is a deliberately constructed classroom pattern.

Practice and evidence: Plot the four pairs with axes and units; write y=3x+1 and verify every point.

Tuesday — Interpret slope and intercept

Learn and model: For y=3x+1, slope 3 represents three additional items per study hour in this invented model; intercept 1 is the prediction at zero hours, not proof that one item would actually be completed.

Practice and evidence: Compute predictions at x=5 and x=0. Explain why prediction outside observed values carries uncertainty.

Wednesday — Compare model and measurement

Learn and model: Fictional observations (1,4),(2,8),(3,9),(4,14) are not perfectly linear. The model y=3x+1 predicts 4,7,10,13, so residuals observed minus predicted are 0,1,−1,1.

Practice and evidence: Build a predicted/observed/residual table and explain why residuals matter.

Thursday — Challenge causal claims

Learn and model: A correlation does not establish causation: students who practice more might differ in experience or instruction. A scatterplot displays association, not the source of the pattern.

Practice and evidence: List two plausible confounders and identify which evidence would strengthen a causal claim.

Friday — Independent modeling

Learn and model: Use fictional points (0,2),(1,4),(2,6),(3,8) to graph a relationship, build a rule and evaluate how well it fits. Distinguish an exact constructed pattern from real observations.

Practice and evidence: Rubric (20): correct graph 5, rule 6, verification 5, evidence limitations 4. Keep official assessment unchanged.
