Week 02 · Social Studies & Civics

Evidence, Annotation, and Ratios

How do evidence and comparison help us understand complex ideas?

Monday: Primary and secondary sources

Focus: Primary and secondary sources.

Learn: A primary source is evidence created during the event or period being studied, such as a diary entry or contemporary map. A secondary source interprets earlier evidence, such as a later historical overview. Source classification depends on the research question.

Practice and evidence: Classify a fictional 1910 journal entry and a 2026 textbook summary about life in 1910. Explain how one source might be primary for one question and secondary for another.

Tuesday: Author, date, purpose

Focus: Author, date, purpose.

Learn: Historical evidence must be read in context. Fictional source A: 'The new bridge cuts our trip in half,' writes a trader in 1910. The speaker may know trade well, but may omit effects on other residents.

Practice and evidence: Annotate the source's author, date, claim, likely audience, and two unanswered questions. Clearly mark the source as a classroom simulation, not an authentic document.

Wednesday: Perspectives on one event

Focus: Perspectives on one event.

Learn: Fictional source B, a river resident in 1910, writes: 'The bridge helps carts cross, but our landing now draws fewer visitors.' Contrasting views can both contain relevant observations, but neither represents every person.

Practice and evidence: Make a comparison table showing evidence, perspective, benefits, and costs for sources A and B. Distinguish each claim from a proven community-wide fact.

Thursday: Maps and corroboration

Focus: Maps and corroboration.

Learn: A map shows spatial information but is made for a purpose. To corroborate a historical claim, compare independent records and check their dates, authors, and limits.

Practice and evidence: Sketch a fictional river, bridge, market, and landing. Use the map to explain how bridge placement could affect different routes. Add a note about what the map cannot prove.

Friday: Historical reasoning with evidence

Focus: Historical reasoning with evidence.

Learn: A defensible interpretation considers source reliability, context, and competing perspectives. A conclusion may be qualified when the available evidence is incomplete.

Practice and evidence: Write a 150-word interpretation: Who benefited from the fictional bridge and who may have faced costs? Reference both simulated sources and one map observation. Rubric: sourcing 5, comparison 5, evidence 6, limitations 4.
