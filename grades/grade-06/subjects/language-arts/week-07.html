Week 07 · Language Arts & Literacy

Coordinate Planes and Geography

How can coordinates help us describe place, movement, and patterns?

Read · Locate · Explain · Revise · Publish

Use original fictional coordinate-map texts to create precise geographic descriptions, connect map and prose evidence, and revise directions that a new reader could misunderstand.

Map-text integration Spatial prepositions Coordinate evidence Independent explanatory writing

Open one lesson at a time. Record student drafts using the existing Academy journal, printable or portfolio; this static page does not automatically save written work.

Monday · Read a map and distinguish its claims

Learning target: Identify the origin, coordinate order, units and map legend before describing locations.

Begin with the original coordinate story
Academy-original narrative · The Map With Two Norths

In a fictional neighborhood mapping club, Leena placed a library at (2,3) and a school at (-1,2) on a square coordinate grid. Each grid step stood for one imaginary block. The positive x-direction pointed east, and the positive y-direction pointed north. The origin (0,0) was the community fountain.

When her partner wrote “The library is two blocks north and three east,” Leena checked the ordered pair. “We agreed x comes first,” she explained, “so the library is two blocks east and three north of the fountain.” The mistake mattered because someone using the description alone could plot a different location.

The map was only a classroom diagram. Its blocks did not correspond to a real city's streets or travel conditions. Leena added a legend describing directions, the units and each symbol before sharing it with a new student.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional story for Grade 6 Week 07; map and destinations are invented.

Worked example: The ordered pair (2,3) locates the library two grid units east and three north of (0,0). The school at (-1,2) lies one unit west and two north of the origin. A coordinate statement must name the agreed axes.

Student practice
  1. Record the origin, x-direction, y-direction and one-block grid unit from the story.
  2. Plot or describe the library (2,3) and school (-1,2), labeling each in words.
  3. Quote the inaccurate statement in paragraph 2 and rewrite it with the correct coordinate order.
  4. Explain why a classroom grid should not be described as a verified city map.

Today's portfolio evidence: Labeled coordinate legend, two correctly described sites and a cited wording correction.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Text-only plotting descriptions may substitute for drawing. Check ordered-pair interpretation and clarity of the agreed directional convention.

Tuesday · Use spatial relationships with reference points

Learning target: Choose accurate location language and revise sentences that hide their point of reference.

Expand lesson, model and student tasks
Academy-original guide · Where Is “Above”?

Spatial language depends on orientation and starting point. On Leena's north-up classroom grid, the school at (-1,2) is west of the fountain, and the library at (2,3) is east of the school. The library is one grid unit north of the school and three grid units east of it. “Near the school” gives much less information than a consistent pair of relative displacements.

Words such as above, below, east of, west of and between can be useful, but a writer should identify the map's orientation. A different rotation of the page could make “to the right” unclear unless the axes were labeled.

A claim about distance should also say whether it measures a horizontal displacement, a vertical displacement, a straight-line distance or a route along drawn grid paths. They are not interchangeable.

Source: Academy-original instructional geography-language text; all locations are fictional.

Worked example: From the school (-1,2) to the library (2,3), the eastward change is 3 and the northward change is 1. The statement describes components of displacement, not a shortest walking route.

Student practice
  1. Write six accurate spatial sentences using both the fountain and school as reference points.
  2. Circle each sentence's reference location and underline its orientation term.
  3. Correct the vague direction “Go right for a while” into a defined grid movement.
  4. Explain why three grid units east and one north are not automatically four blocks of real walking distance.

Today's portfolio evidence: Six source-correct sentences, a reference-word annotation and a revised directional statement.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Students should identify the starting point every time they change frames of reference. Avoid implying a classroom displacement is a real navigation route.

Wednesday · Connect an informational passage to a map

Learning target: Translate a route description into ordered pairs and evaluate whether the text matches the map.

Expand lesson, model and student tasks
Academy-original informational text · The Garden Delivery Note

The fictional garden club began at a meeting point (-2,1). Its drawing placed a packet shelf at (1,1), a shade tree at (1,4), and a tool table at (-2,4). A written note instructed volunteers to walk three grid units east from the meeting point to the shelf, then three units north to the tree. It described movements on the diagram rather than an accessible or verified outdoor path.

A second draft said to walk “three units west to the shelf.” That contradicts the coordinates: moving from x = -2 to x = 1 increases x by three units. The club checked the location symbols and corrected the note before producing the final explanatory paragraph.

The diagram included no real-world obstacles, sidewalks, surface conditions or travel distances. Its purpose was to show how map evidence supports or contradicts a spatial description.

Source: Academy-original fictional map-and-text exercise, Grade 6 Week 07.

Worked example: From (-2,1) to (1,1), x increases by three while y stays 1: three grid units east. From (1,1) to (1,4), y increases by three: three north. Both are diagram movements, not proven real routes.

Student practice
  1. Construct a simple grid or text-only listing of the four fictional sites.
  2. Cite the sentence that is incompatible with the plotted coordinates, and explain the error.
  3. Write a four-sentence description of the first two route segments with exact starting and ending pairs.
  4. Propose one limitation statement that should accompany the fictional diagram if someone tries to treat it as a real town map.

Today's portfolio evidence: Map-text consistency table, corrected route paragraph and source limitation.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

A clear student paragraph should mention both start and end coordinates. Grade 6 literacy evaluates interpretation and explanation, not actual geographic routing.

Thursday · Write and test directions for a new reader

Learning target: Draft a complete explanatory route guide using sequential transitions, units and a consistent legend.

Expand lesson, model and student tasks
Academy-original editor note · Directions Without Guessing

A test reader followed the instruction “From the fountain, go up, then across.” The author had forgotten to state the orientation or destination. The reader could not tell whether “up” meant north on the map, uphill on a real path, or toward the top of a printed page.

The author rewrote the sentence: “On the fictional north-up grid, start at (0,0), travel two grid units east to (2,0), then three units north to the library at (2,3).” A precise guide states the reference frame, distances and endpoints. The revised text does not claim the route exists outside the drawing.

Source: Academy-original procedural-language note.

Worked example: Before: “Walk over there from the school.” After: “On the north-up drawing, begin at (-1,2), travel three grid units east to (2,2), then one unit north to the library at (2,3).” State that the described segments are imaginary grid movements.

Student practice
  1. Draft a 170–230-word guide to at least three fictional locations using ordered pairs and the agreed axes.
  2. Define the map's origin, scale and symbols before the sequence of instructions.
  3. Identify two ambiguities by following only your own writing; save the original and revised phrases.
  4. Add an accessible text-only description so a reader can reproduce the grid without seeing your diagram.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 170–230-word route explanation with legend, accessible text and documented two-step revision.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Peer testing is optional; an honest self-test is sufficient. Focus on writing independence, sentence sequence and avoiding unsupported real-world access claims.

Friday · Publish a map-linked explanatory guide

Learning target: Produce a reliable map-text artifact with evidence, precise locations and reflection.

Expand lesson, model and student tasks
Academy-original editorial challenge · The Shortcut Nobody Checked

“The library at (2,3) is only one real-world block from the school at (-1,2), so our fictional map proves it is the safest walking route.”

The invented claim wrongly treats coordinates as a verified street route. Relative x and y differences are 3 and 1 grid units, and the diagram includes no information about actual safety or accessibility.

Worked example: A defensible caption: “In the fictional north-up grid, the library is three units east and one north of the school. The chart demonstrates relative location; it does not establish actual street distance, travel time or safety.”

Student practice
  1. Identify three unsupported parts of the invented shortcut claim and cite the original coordinates.
  2. Rewrite the claim as a precise diagram-only caption.
  3. File Thursday's first and revised guide with the coordinate legend and map-text consistency chart.
  4. Reflect in four sentences on how reference points, spatial prepositions and source limits improved clarity.

Today's portfolio evidence: Final fictional geography guide, corrected caption, revision trace and reflection. Educator practice rubric /20: spatial accuracy 6, prose clarity 5, map-text evidence 5, revision 4. Not an official grade.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Preserve formal Week 07 assessment and the 80% mastery gate. A fictional grid is not a verified route; keep the learner's writing in the existing portfolio workflow.

Writing and map-analysis coaching are developmental only: no automatic grades or mastery. The existing Week 07 assessment, student records and official 80% gate remain authoritative.
