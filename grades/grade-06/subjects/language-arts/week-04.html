Week 04 · Language Arts & Literacy

Percent, Proportion, and Civic Decisions

How can percentages help a community make informed decisions?

Read · Check · Explain · Recommend · Revise

A civic reading laboratory using an Academy-original library-hours survey. Learn to identify claims, convert survey counts into precise language, detect overstatements, and write a fair recommendation grounded in the actual sources.

Expand one lesson at a time. Use your existing learning journal, printable packet or student portfolio for written responses; this page does not automatically save essays.

Monday · Read a survey report and test its claims

Learning target: Identify a report’s claim, two supporting details, and the precise group to which its numbers apply.

Begin with the original survey report
Academy-original informational report · The Library Hours Question

At the fictional Alder School, a student committee wanted to know whether classmates would appreciate one extra hour of library access on Wednesdays. The committee asked students to complete a voluntary paper survey during lunch. Exactly 50 students returned completed forms; the school had more students than the 50 who replied.

Of those 50 respondents, 30 selected “I would like an extra Wednesday library hour,” 15 selected “I would not,” and 5 selected “I am not sure.” The committee wrote that 30 out of 50 responding students favored the extra hour. That is 60% of the respondents, not necessarily 60% of everyone enrolled at Alder.

A librarian noted that an extra hour might help students find quiet reading time. Another staff member wondered how supervision and transportation would work. The committee had not asked respondents about travel, and the questionnaire did not establish whether an added hour would improve reading performance.

Before giving the school a recommendation, the committee planned to seek a wider sample, gather practical information, and listen to students with different schedules. A useful report, they reasoned, should state both what the data show and what the survey has not established.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional report, Grade 6 Week 04. All people, institutions and numbers are invented.

Reading model: The statement “30 of the 50 survey respondents favored an extra hour” is supported by paragraph 2. “Most Alder students favor it” is not established because not all students responded. An honest source note includes both the count and the surveyed group.

Student practice
  1. Identify the report’s purpose and write one narrowly supported claim about the responses.
  2. Create a three-row table showing Yes (30), No (15) and Unsure (5) and name the denominator.
  3. Cite two different paragraphs: one giving the response data and one describing a limitation.
  4. Explain why the voluntary lunch survey cannot show what every Alder student wants.

Today's portfolio evidence: Source-labeled survey table, one precise claim, two paragraph citations and an evidence limitation.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Use the phrase 'of the respondents' consistently. Remind students that imaginary survey counts are reading exercises, not public-opinion findings.

Tuesday · Explain percentages in an informational caption

Learning target: Turn fictional counts into accurate percentages while preserving the population and question.

Expand today's original reading and practice
Academy-original data note · A Different Survey

For a separate fictional class reading project, 40 students answered a different question: “Would you join a weekly book-discussion group?” Ten said yes, 20 said no, and 10 were undecided. Ten out of forty equals one quarter, or 25% of this group’s responses.

This small survey was conducted in a different group and asked about a book-discussion group rather than Wednesday library hours. The two surveys should not be blended into one claim. A chart caption should state the question, the count, the denominator, and the fictional source.

A reader who sees “25% support libraries” would reasonably misunderstand the finding. The 25% describes only students who selected yes to the book-discussion question. It does not measure who likes the library, who uses it, or what all students think.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional data note, Grade 6 Week 04.

Worked caption: “In the fictional book-discussion survey, 10 of 40 respondents (25%) said they would join a weekly group (Academy data note, paragraph 1).” The caption does not substitute a different question or group.

Student practice
  1. Write an accurate four-part caption for the book-discussion survey: question, fraction, percentage, and source.
  2. Calculate the 30/50 library-hours response percentage and the 10/40 book-discussion response percentage.
  3. Explain why the percentages should not be treated as results of the same question or student group.
  4. Revise the misleading headline “Only 25% of all Alder students want a library” with a source-faithful statement.

Today's portfolio evidence: Two accurate source captions with denominators, percentages, and a correction of a misleading headline.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Mathematics verifies basic arithmetic, but the English learning target is precision of meaning and source context. Do not carry these practice values into the official mathematics grade.

Wednesday · Detect words that exaggerate findings

Learning target: Distinguish a persuasive interpretation from what source documents actually support.

Expand today's original reading and practice
Academy-original opinion · Every Student Wants More Hours

“The student survey proves everybody wants a longer library day. If we listen to the numbers, Alder must extend the hours immediately. Anyone who asks about staffing is ignoring students.”

Academy-original response · Numbers Need Context

“Thirty of fifty respondents favored the extra Wednesday hour, so there is reason to explore the idea. The school should also ask more students and consider supervision and transportation before deciding. The original survey did not measure how many students could actually attend.”

The two writers discuss the same fictional question. One uses certainty and personal criticism; the other identifies a supportive finding while recognizing unknowns. Readers can favor more access without overstating the evidence.

Claim audit: Words like “everybody,” “proves,” and “must” go beyond a voluntary survey of 50. An accurate revision begins “Among the 50 respondents, 30 favored …” and treats staffing as a practical question, not proof that someone dislikes students.

Student practice
  1. Find three phrases in the first opinion that overstate the survey results.
  2. Write a corrected sentence for each exaggerated phrase, citing the report’s paragraph 2 or 3.
  3. Compare the two writers’ purposes and identify one fair concern each raises.
  4. Prepare a two-column list labeled “Source says” and “Writer assumes,” with at least three entries.

Today's portfolio evidence: Three revisions of overstatements, a source-versus-assumption chart, and a short comparison of author purposes.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Encourage students to criticize the reasoning rather than attacking the opinion writer. A persuasive purpose does not excuse incorrect attribution.

Thursday · Draft a measured civic recommendation

Learning target: Combine numerical evidence, human perspectives and source limitations in a reasoned paragraph.

Expand today's original reading and practice
Academy-original pair of perspectives · Two Wednesday Schedules

Student comment: “After robotics club, I could use quiet library time to catch up on reading. I would try the extra hour.”

Family comment: “My student takes the earlier bus. Before extending the schedule, I would want to know how people could get home and who would supervise.”

Neither individual comment represents every student or family. Compare the comments to The Library Hours Question; each offers context that a count alone does not supply.

Worked argument: “The committee has a reason to investigate a longer Wednesday library hour because 30 of 50 respondents supported it (report, paragraph 2). However, it should study supervision and transportation before promising access to all students (report, paragraph 3; family comment).”

Student practice
  1. Draft 160–220 original words recommending a next step for Alder, using the survey and at least one comment.
  2. State the 30/50 result accurately and name the group represented.
  3. Include one fair counterconsideration, then explain how your proposal would address it.
  4. Highlight one unsupported adjective or generalization in your draft and revise it without weakening the core claim.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 160–220-word civic recommendation with two source labels, a limitation and a visible before-and-after revision.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Accept different proposals that fit the evidence. Evaluate clarity, source precision and consideration of practical access, not students’ favored policy.

Friday · Correct a misleading poster and reflect

Learning target: Independently interpret fictional data, revise persuasion and preserve a clear learning portfolio.

Expand today's original reading and practice
Academy-original poster challenge · 60% of Everyone?

“Alder students voted 60% yes, so 60% of the entire school will use the library on Wednesdays!”

Check this invented poster against the original voluntary survey and the unanswered practical questions. Correct claims about the surveyed group, actual attendance and universal preferences.

Accurate correction: “In the voluntary survey, 30 of 50 respondents (60%) favored an extra library hour. The survey does not tell us how many would actually attend or what every student thinks.”

Student practice
  1. Identify two separate unsupported conclusions in the poster and show where the report limits them.
  2. Rewrite the poster in two accurate sentences with fraction, percentage and source label.
  3. Save Thursday’s first and revised recommendations, plus the comparison chart and your corrected poster.
  4. Reflect in four sentences on how changing one word, such as “everyone,” can make a civic claim more reliable.

Today's portfolio evidence: Corrected poster, evidence chart, original and revised recommendation, and reflection. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: accurate claims 4, numbers and source notes 6, reasoning 4, fair counterconsideration 3, revision 3. This is not an official grade.
