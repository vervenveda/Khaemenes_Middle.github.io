Week 01 · Language Arts & Literacy

Middle School Scholar Launch

How do middle school scholars organize independence, evidence, and responsibility?

English Evidence Journey · Week 01

Read two Academy-original texts. Practice evidence, inference, summary, and responsible revision, then prepare a teacher-reviewed portfolio sample.

Two text citations Observation versus inference Main idea and paraphrase Claim → Evidence → Reasoning

Open one day at a time. Students can use existing portfolio/printable tools to preserve work; these lesson pages do not automatically save written responses.

Monday · Discover the story and its evidence

Learning target: Cite two different actions that support a defensible claim about responsibility.

Start reading and learning
Academy-original narrative: The Missing Map Key

Mina arrived early for class. She checked her assignment list and noticed the group map lacked a key. Before anyone asked, she labeled its symbols. Later she wrote down one question for tomorrow.

The map marked a garden in green, a walking path in gold, and a stream in blue. The colors could confuse anyone unfamiliar with the neighborhood. Mina checked the assignment directions before adding a legend. When Jonah asked whether they should wait for the teacher, Mina showed him the instructions: every symbol needed an explanation.

During review, another student noticed that two paths had been drawn in the same color. Instead of claiming she knew which path was safe, Mina marked the uncertain line with a question and invited the group to consult the original route plan. The map was not finished, but the group now knew what it needed to verify.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fiction, Week 01.

Worked example: Model: “Mina helps make information clear” is a claim. The legend and her decision to check an uncertain path are two different pieces of evidence. Explain what each action shows rather than repeating the claim.

Student practice
  1. Find two supporting actions and identify their paragraphs.
  2. Write an arguable claim about responsibility.
  3. Quote or paraphrase each action precisely.
  4. Explain why arriving early alone does not prove that Mina always plans ahead.

Today’s portfolio evidence: Four sentences: claim, two cited details, and an explanation.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Check that every inference is supported by the actual text; avoid interpreting Mina’s motives as a proven fact.

Tuesday · Test observations and inferences

Learning target: Distinguish stated details from interpretations and consider an alternate explanation.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Close reading: revisit The Missing Map Key

An observation is directly visible in the story. An inference interprets observations; an alternative explanation keeps that interpretation fair. A single detail can support more than one reasonable explanation.

Worked example: Worked example: “Mina wrote a legend beside the map” is stated. “Mina wants the map to be understandable” is inferred. “She was following the teacher’s directions” is another plausible explanation.

Student practice
  1. Make a two-column chart with three observations and two inferences.
  2. Cite the paragraph that supports each inference.
  3. Propose one competing explanation for Mina’s choice to check the original route plan.
  4. Explain why the story does not justify saying Mina knows the safest path.

Today’s portfolio evidence: An evidence-supported observation/inference chart and alternate reading.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Ask the learner to distinguish what the author actually says from what a careful reader reasonably believes.

Wednesday · Understand nonfiction and its central idea

Learning target: Identify the main idea, summarize accurately, and paraphrase with attribution.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Academy-original nonfiction: Planning Without Guesswork

A learning plan organizes tasks by urgency and due date. Breaking assignments into smaller steps makes large projects more manageable. Reviewing the plan daily helps students catch missed tasks. A plan should remain flexible when circumstances change.

A team writing “finish the map” as its only task may overlook important details. A stronger plan might say: “verify path labels,” “explain the symbols,” and “ask a classmate to review the final version.” Those steps are concrete enough to check.

A plan does not solve every unknown. If a route has conflicting labels, the group must investigate or seek help rather than inventing an answer. Responsible planning includes acknowledging what remains unfinished.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original informational reading, Week 01.

Worked example: Model: Topic = managing schoolwork. Central idea = a useful plan breaks work into checkable steps and adapts when questions arise. The map example supports that idea; it is not the whole central idea.

Student practice
  1. Write the central idea in one sentence.
  2. Select three details that support it.
  3. Summarize the passage in three new sentences.
  4. Paraphrase the final paragraph and identify the Academy original as the source.

Today’s portfolio evidence: One central idea, three specific details, a concise summary, and an attributed paraphrase.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Check whether the answer explains the whole informational passage rather than just its map example.

Thursday · Compose and revise a reasoned paragraph

Learning target: Connect evidence from both genres and improve meaning through revision.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Across two genres

Return to both readings. The narrative demonstrates a character deciding what to check; the informational reading explains planning choices. They treat related ideas through different forms.

Worked example: Model: “Mina records her uncertainty instead of inventing an answer (story, paragraph 3). The informational reading also argues that a learning plan must leave room to investigate uncertain details (informational text, paragraph 3).” Add your own reasoning explaining why the connection matters.

Student practice
  1. Answer: How can students show independence while still asking questions?
  2. Draft 120–180 original words using a detail from each reading.
  3. Include source and paragraph locators.
  4. Revise two sentences: one vague explanation and one weak transition, and preserve the before-and-after versions.

Today’s portfolio evidence: A supported paragraph and a visible revision record.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Give feedback on reasoning before editing conventions. Two sources must contribute, not just be mentioned.

Friday · Compare, reflect, and build portfolio evidence

Learning target: Demonstrate independent source comparison and meaningful revision.

Expand the daily lesson and practice
Independent comparison

A narrative allows the reader to infer attitudes from actions. An informational text states a general idea directly. Explain how the two original readings differ while showing what both suggest about checking uncertain information.

Worked example: Model comparison: Mina’s hesitation over the conflicting route label illustrates careful checking; the informational passage directly advises teams to investigate missing information.

Student practice
  1. Identify an inference about Mina and the central idea of the nonfiction.
  2. Use two cited details to explain a meaningful difference between the texts.
  3. Revise the Thursday paragraph and attach the original and revised versions to your portfolio.
  4. Reflect in 3–4 sentences: Which evidence made your thinking more precise?

Today’s portfolio evidence: Teacher-reviewed evidence rubric (20 points): claim 4, accurate text evidence 6, reasoning 4, organization 3, revision/conventions 3. Sixteen points is a practice target, not verified digital mastery.

Teacher and family facilitator notes

Keep teacher feedback and the official verified 80% assessment entirely separate.

This week’s instructional practice and teacher rubrics never replace the verified 80% Academy assessment engine. Learner work remains separate from academic mastery.
