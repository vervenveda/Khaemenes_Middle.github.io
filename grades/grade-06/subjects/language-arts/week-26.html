Week 26 · Language Arts & Literacy

Classical Civilizations and Civic Ideas

How did classical societies influence civic ideas and public life?

Monday: Reading public speeches

Focus: Reading public speeches.

Learn: A civic speech has a speaker, audience, purpose and historical context. A surviving speech may be edited or translated.

Practice and evidence: Annotate an original fictional assembly speech: identify its claim, intended audience and two persuasive choices.

Tuesday: Compare civic voices

Focus: Compare civic voices.

Learn: Written accounts of Greek poleis and Rome represent particular voices, not everyone who lived there; avoid treating citizenship as universally inclusive.

Practice and evidence: Compare two short invented accounts from a citizen and a resident excluded from voting. Write two similarities and two differences.

Wednesday: Trace claims to evidence

Focus: Trace claims to evidence.

Learn: A strong historical argument distinguishes evidence from inference and says where quotations or translations came from.

Practice and evidence: Make a claim-evidence-reasoning chart about whether a fictional town assembly served all residents.

Thursday: Revise a balanced argument

Focus: Revise a balanced argument.

Learn: A counterclaim improves an argument when it receives fair treatment. Do not assume modern democratic meanings fit all ancient institutions.

Practice and evidence: Draft a paragraph arguing for or against a fictional civic proposal; answer one informed counterargument.

Friday: Publish an evidence-based civic essay

Focus: Publish an evidence-based civic essay.

Learn: An explanatory essay should establish context, cite the assigned texts, acknowledge limits and reach a defensible conclusion.

Practice and evidence: Submit a 180-word fictional-source analysis with a claim, two cited details, a counterclaim and revision. Rubric: context 5, evidence 7, reasoning 5, reflection 3.
