Week 03 · Language Arts & Literacy

Rates, Unit Rates, and Informational Text

How can rates and text structures explain change?

Read · Structure · Note · Explain · Revise

Read Academy-original informational writing about a fictional cycling club. Notice how comparison, sequence, and explanation organize a text. Use precise vocabulary, trustworthy notes, and unit-rate evidence to revise an original response.

Open one day at a time. Written responses should be recorded with the student's existing journal, portfolio, or printable packet. These lesson cards do not automatically save writing.

Monday · Read informational text and identify its structure

Learning target: Identify the central idea and explain how a writer organizes comparisons rather than unsupported causes.

Start Week 03 with the original report
Academy-original report · Two Afternoons on the Trail

A neighborhood cycling club recorded distance and time during two imaginary practice rides. On Monday, club members traveled 12 kilometers in 3 hours. On Tuesday, they traveled 15 kilometers in 3 hours. The club compared both distance and time before writing a short report for families. Its notebook did not say why the distance differed.

The report began with a purpose: help readers understand how two recorded rides compared. A small table listed each day, distance, and duration. Because each ride lasted three hours, Tuesday's group covered three more kilometers in the same amount of recorded time. The report did not claim all riders kept the same speed throughout either ride.

Several club members suggested explanations. One thought Tuesday's route might have had fewer hills; another wondered whether the group had stopped less often. The report treated these ideas as questions, not findings. Without more information about terrain, stops, and weather, it could not establish a cause.

The club ended with a recommendation: when describing activity, writers should give quantities with units, identify what was measured, and name any important information they lack. A comparison can be useful without pretending to answer every question.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional informational report for English 6, Week 03; the cycling figures are invented.

Worked reading: Topic: two bicycle rides. Central idea: fair comparisons need both distance and duration, and recorded results do not establish causes. The report uses a comparison structure in paragraph 2 and describes untested explanations in paragraph 3.

Student practice
  1. State the central idea in one sentence, then select two different supporting details with paragraph locators.
  2. Label paragraph 1 as context, paragraph 2 as comparison, paragraph 3 as possible explanations, and paragraph 4 as conclusion; explain one structural choice.
  3. Make a two-row chart showing each day's distance, duration, and units without adding missing information.
  4. Write one question that would help the club test a possible cause of the difference.

Today's portfolio evidence: A central-idea statement, a four-paragraph structural map, a cited comparison table, and one research question.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Invite the learner to notice the difference between a text reporting measured facts and speculating about causes. Do not turn the English analysis into a replacement Math assessment.

Tuesday · Compare text structures and signal words

Learning target: Recognize how comparison, sequence, and cause-and-effect language shape a reader's understanding.

Expand today's lesson and practice
Academy-original explanatory text · How a Report Guides the Reader

To prepare a clear report, the club first recorded the date, distance, and time. Next, its members checked the entries for missing units. Then they placed the two rides side by side in a table. Finally, they wrote a paragraph explaining what the table showed. Those transition words reveal a sequence of actions.

In a different paragraph the club used comparison terms: “both rides lasted three hours,” “Tuesday covered more distance,” and “the time was equal.” These phrases help readers judge similarities and differences. If the writer instead said “Tuesday was faster because the wind helped,” the word because would introduce a causal explanation that the report had not verified.

Writers sometimes combine structures in one text. A sequence explains what a group did, a comparison explains how outcomes differed, and a cause-and-effect claim attempts to explain why something happened. A careful reader checks whether the evidence supports each structure's claims.

Source: Academy-original instructional nonfiction, English 6, Week 03.

Worked example: “First” and “next” indicate sequence; “both” and “more than” indicate comparison. “Because” may signal a proposed cause, but the word alone is not evidence that the cause is correct.

Student practice
  1. Underline four sequence signal words in paragraph 1 and write what each connects.
  2. Find three comparison phrases in paragraph 2 and describe the relationship they express.
  3. Rewrite one unsupported “because” sentence into a responsible statement about uncertainty.
  4. Explain why a report can use both sequence and comparison without becoming confusing.

Today's portfolio evidence: A signal-word annotation chart, a structure comparison, and one revised causal overclaim.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Have students identify the purpose of a paragraph before naming the structure. Terms like comparison and sequence describe organization, not whether a writer is truthful.

Wednesday · Take accurate source notes

Learning target: Use a structured note method to preserve exact quantities and distinguish summaries from quotations.

Expand today's lesson and practice
Academy-original note-taking model · Questions and Evidence

A reader preparing a short explanation creates two columns. On the left go questions: “What was measured?” “What changed?” “What is unknown?” On the right go short sourced facts: “Monday: 12 km in 3 h,” “Tuesday: 15 km in 3 h,” and “Conditions such as hills and stops not recorded.” The reader adds a brief summary below: “The second ride covered more distance in the same recorded time, but the causes are unknown.”

Good notes retain units and source context. If the reader writes only “12, 15, 3,” the numbers lose meaning. If a reader writes “The club proved Tuesday was easier,” the notes introduce an unsupported claim. Notes can be concise without removing accuracy.

Source: Academy-original model derived from the fictional report Two Afternoons on the Trail.

Worked note: Question: “What was recorded?” Detail: “Monday, 12 kilometers in 3 hours” (report, paragraph 1). Summary: “The club compared two observations.” An exact quotation needs quotation marks; a paraphrase keeps the meaning with fresh wording.

Student practice
  1. Create a Cornell-style two-column page containing three research questions and at least five concise evidence notes.
  2. Write the exact source and paragraph next to the 12 km / 3 h and 15 km / 3 h records.
  3. Select one important sentence to quote accurately, then paraphrase its meaning without copying it.
  4. Identify and repair two misleading sample notes: “Tuesday was safer” and “Both rides were 15 kilometers.”

Today's portfolio evidence: A source-labeled two-column note sheet, a true quotation, an accurate paraphrase, and corrected notes.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Look for meaningful notes rather than copied paragraphs. The student should be able to reconstruct the argument from their own notes with the source still identifiable.

Thursday · Explain unit rates in clear prose

Learning target: Define technical language in context and translate calculations into cautious, comprehensible sentences.

Expand today's lesson and practice
Academy-original explanatory sidebar · Distance Per Hour

A unit rate describes a quantity for one unit of another quantity. In the cycling example, Monday's average distance per recorded hour is 12 ÷ 3 = 4 kilometers per hour. Tuesday's is 15 ÷ 3 = 5 kilometers per hour. The unit “kilometers per hour” tells the reader what the numbers describe.

The numbers summarize distance divided by time; they do not show a cyclist's exact speed at every moment. If the riders paused or traveled at different speeds, the average would still express the full trip's distance over its reported duration.

Source: Academy-original explanation using fictional figures, Week 03.

Revision model: Imprecise: “Tuesday was faster by 1.” Precise: “The club's recorded average distance per hour was 5 km/h Tuesday versus 4 km/h Monday, a difference of 1 km/h.” The second sentence identifies the quantity and unit without inventing a cause.

Student practice
  1. Define distance, duration, average rate, and unit rate in student-friendly language using the text.
  2. Write a two-sentence comparison using 4 km/h and 5 km/h with exact units and source attribution.
  3. Explain what this average comparison cannot show about stops and conditions.
  4. Draft a 110–160-word informational explanation that introduces the text and accurately interprets both rates.

Today's portfolio evidence: A technical glossary and a 110–160-word cited explanation distinguishing average rate from unobserved conditions.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Students should narrate quantities with full units and avoid making unsupported speed or safety claims. Correct conceptual vocabulary as part of writing practice.

Friday · Synthesize, revise, and preserve learning evidence

Learning target: Integrate text structure, notes, and rate evidence in a polished explanatory paragraph.

Expand today's lesson and practice
Independent review · What the numbers actually support

A fictional newsletter states: “Tuesday's group traveled farther in the same time; therefore the route must have been easier.” A classmate replies, “The report provides enough information to calculate an average rate but not enough to determine why the rides differed.”

Choose the interpretation best supported by Two Afternoons on the Trail and explain why. The same report can support a meaningful numerical comparison while leaving other questions open.

Worked correction: “Tuesday's recorded average was 5 km/h, higher than Monday's 4 km/h (report, paragraph 1), but the report does not test whether the route was easier or why the results differed (paragraph 3).”

Student practice
  1. Write a one-sentence central idea followed by three evidence-based explanation sentences.
  2. Create a 160–210-word synthesis using the report, the explanatory sidebar, and a paragraph locator for each factual detail.
  3. Identify and revise two words or phrases that overstate what the records prove; show before/after versions.
  4. Save the notes, original and revised explanation, and a four-sentence reflection about how precise units improved your writing.

Today's portfolio evidence: Cited 160–210-word synthesis, note sheet, evidence chart, revision trace, and reflection. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: central idea 4, accurate evidence and units 6, text structure/reasoning 4, revision 3, attribution 3. Not an official course grade.
