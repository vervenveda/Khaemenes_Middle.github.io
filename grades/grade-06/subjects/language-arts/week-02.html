Week 02 · Language Arts & Literacy

Evidence, Annotation, and Ratios

How do evidence and comparison help us understand complex ideas?

Read · Annotate · Cite · Revise

Evidence, Annotation, and Ratios · Read two original garden texts. Annotate accurately, distinguish quotation from paraphrase, interpret a comparison fairly, and revise an evidence-based explanation.

Purposeful annotation Quotation and attribution Paraphrase versus summary Fair comparisons and evidence

Expand one day at a time. Record written responses in your existing journal or weekly packet; these static pages do not save essays automatically.

Monday · Annotate the garden story

Learning target: Mark observations, essential vocabulary, questions, and one inference using a key.

Begin with the original reading
Academy-original narrative · The Window Garden

At sunrise, Eli checked the community garden. The seedlings leaned toward a sunlit window. Eli wrote down the date, described the leaves, and measured growth before sharing observations with the group.

The garden club had planted two trays of beans ten days earlier. Tray A sat beside the classroom window, while Tray B sat on a nearby shelf under a small lamp. Eli noticed that some stems in Tray A curved toward the window. In a notebook he drew the direction of their bend and measured three stems, but he did not claim the light was the only reason for their shape.

When Maya suggested moving Tray B closer to the window, Eli asked the team to record the lamp schedule, watering amounts, and location before making changes. “If we move everything at once,” he said, “how will we know which change mattered?” Maya agreed that their notes needed to be clearer. They wrote a question for tomorrow and labeled their diagram.

At the end of the meeting, Eli wrote: “We observed stems leaning toward the window. We have not tested why they lean.” His notes were not a finished explanation, but the group knew exactly what to investigate next.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional narrative, Grade 6 Week 02. The classroom observations are invented for reading instruction, not a scientific experiment.

Think-aloud example: Mark “measured three stems” with E (evidence), “lamp schedule” with V (vocabulary/context), and “What else might influence the stems?” with Q (question). Mark “Eli is careful about conclusions” as I (inference), not a fact stated by the author.

Student practice
  1. Create a four-symbol annotation key: E for direct evidence, Q for questions, V for vocabulary, and I for inference.
  2. Annotate one sentence from each of the four paragraphs, recording why it matters.
  3. Quote two details demonstrating Eli's care and cite their paragraph numbers.
  4. Write an alternative interpretation of why the team postponed moving the tray; explain whether the passage supports it.

Today's portfolio evidence: An annotated passage with four distinct symbols and a claim supported by two precisely located details.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Students should avoid treating fictional observations as proof of the scientific cause of plant growth. Assess whether annotations reveal thinking, not just highlighted words.

Tuesday · Quote, paraphrase, and cite

Learning target: Use an accurate quotation with an attribution phrase and explain its significance.

Expand lesson, examples, and student activities
Read a short passage closely

From The Window Garden, paragraph 3: “If we move everything at once,” he said, “how will we know which change mattered?”

A quotation reproduces exact words. A paraphrase restates a detail in new wording while maintaining the source's meaning. Both require a source note. A reader should be able to distinguish your interpretation from the author's actual words.

Worked example: Eli worries that altering many conditions at once will make conclusions unreliable (Khaemenes Academy, “The Window Garden,” paragraph 3). The concern supports an interpretation that Eli values careful observation. This is a paraphrase with explanation, not an exact quotation.

Student practice
  1. Copy the question from paragraph 3 exactly once, with opening and closing quotation marks.
  2. Write an attribution phrase naming Eli and the Academy-original story.
  3. Explain in two sentences how that quotation supports the idea of careful investigation.
  4. Correct this inaccurate quote: “If we move one thing, we'll know what worked.” Explain why misquoting changes the evidence.

Today's portfolio evidence: One exact, attributed quotation and a two-sentence explanation, plus a correction of inaccurate quoting.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Check exact wording and punctuation first; then require the student to explain why the quote matters. Citation labels should be simple but consistent.

Wednesday · Paraphrase without changing the meaning

Learning target: Distinguish a useful paraphrase from a copied sentence, distorted statement, or overgeneralized summary.

Expand lesson, examples, and student activities
Academy-original informational text · What a Fair Comparison Shows

Comparisons help readers recognize differences, but the numbers must refer to the same kind of quantity. Imagine that a fictional garden club counts 4 bent seedlings among 8 in one tray and 6 among 12 in another. Although the second tray has more bent seedlings, both have one-half of their seedlings bent. Comparing only the number of bent stems would give an incomplete picture.

The totals in the trays are not the same, so the group must compare portions of each total. A ratio describes the relationship between two quantities. It does not explain the cause of a plant's shape, and the invented numbers cannot establish what would happen in another classroom.

A careful writer states what the numbers show and also what they do not show. Source notes help another reader find the example being discussed. A fair paraphrase keeps these limits intact rather than presenting an observation as proof.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original instructional nonfiction, Grade 6 Week 02. All quantities are fictional.

Model paraphrase: In the Academy's hypothetical example, half the seedlings in each tray lean, even though the trays contain different numbers of seedlings (informational text, paragraph 1). “Tray B's plants bend more because of its lamp” would distort what the source actually establishes.

Student practice
  1. Write one sentence stating the informational passage's central idea.
  2. Paraphrase the comparison of 4 of 8 and 6 of 12 using your own syntax, and identify the paragraph.
  3. Explain why “6 is greater than 4, so the second tray has a greater share of bent stems” is misleading.
  4. Summarize all three paragraphs in two or three original sentences, preserving the limits of the fictional data.

Today's portfolio evidence: An attributed paraphrase, a two-to-three-sentence summary, and an explanation of fair numerical comparison.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

The mathematics is a reading-comprehension support. Emphasize the source's distinction between a count and a proportion, without substituting this task for the Math course assessment.

Thursday · Evaluate the strongest evidence

Learning target: Support a precise interpretation and reject claims the two texts do not justify.

Expand lesson, examples, and student activities
Compare claims across narrative and nonfiction

Use The Window Garden and What a Fair Comparison Shows. Both texts discuss careful use of evidence. One shows what a character chooses to do; the other explains how numbers and limits affect interpretations.

Claim A: Eli measures stems and asks the group to record conditions before moving trays.

Claim B: Eli likes sunny mornings more than his classmates.

Claim C: The invented garden ratios prove a particular lighting method causes stems to bend.

Worked example: Claim A is directly supported by the narrative. Claims B and C go beyond what the sources say. A stronger interpretation is “Careful investigators record conditions and compare evidence before explaining a change.”

Student practice
  1. Classify Claims A, B, and C as directly stated, inference, or unsupported and justify each decision.
  2. Select two strong details from the narrative and one from the nonfiction to explain careful comparison.
  3. Draft a 140–190-word claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph with simple source and paragraph citations.
  4. Revise one unsupported sentence and one quotation that needs more explanation; keep original and revised versions.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 140–190-word evidence-based comparison, two documented revisions, and at least three source locators.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Check whether the writing includes accurate explanation and fair limitations, not just three quotations. Praise narrowing an exaggerated claim.

Friday · Demonstrate annotation and revision growth

Learning target: Independently annotate, write with source integrity, and reflect on revisions.

Expand lesson, examples, and student activities
Independent evidence check

“The club noticed different stem shapes and wrote down the conditions it had not yet tested. The group planned to compare observations before giving an explanation.”

This is an Academy-original short recap, not a substitute for either full text. When citing evidence, return to the actual original paragraphs rather than using this recap as if it contained everything.

Self-check model: A supported explanation points to Eli's note about unknown conditions and the nonfiction's warning about comparing counts. It does not claim the invented garden observations establish plant biology.

Student practice
  1. Annotate the independent recap with an observation, a question, and a supported inference.
  2. Write one exact quotation and one paraphrase from the full readings, citing both sources.
  3. Submit the original and revised Thursday paragraph and explain which changed sentence improves the reasoning.
  4. Reflect in 3–4 sentences on when a quotation is more useful than a paraphrase.

Today's portfolio evidence: Annotated recap, source-accurate quotation and paraphrase, revised paragraph, and reflection. Suggested teacher rubric /20: annotation 4, quotation/paraphrase integrity 6, reasoning 4, revision 3, reflection 3. This is developmental feedback, not verified course mastery.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance
