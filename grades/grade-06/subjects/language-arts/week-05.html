Week 05 · Language Arts & Literacy

Fraction Division and Procedural Writing

How can models explain division when the numbers are fractions?

Read · Clarify · Model · Test · Publish

Learn to write independently usable procedures using two Academy-original fraction-strip readings, exact terminology, careful transitions, accessible descriptions and documented revisions.

Purpose and audience Precise transitions Materials and units Peer testing and revision

Open one daily card at a time. Save drafts in your existing Academy journal or portfolio; these lesson pages do not automatically persist written answers.

Monday · Read a procedural narrative

Learning target: Explain how reader, purpose, materials and precision determine whether instructions can be followed.

Begin with the original reading
Academy-original narrative · The Fraction-Strip Mix-Up

Samira wanted to show a classmate how to build a fraction strip. Her first directions said: “Draw a line, make some pieces, and shade the amount.” The classmate divided the strip into unequal segments and shaded a random part. “I followed your instructions,” the classmate said. Samira realized that she had written what she understood, not what a new reader needed.

She rewrote the goal: represent three fourths divided by one fourth using equal-sized parts. Her materials included a pencil, ruler and paper, or a digital drawing tool. She specified one 12-centimeter bar partitioned into four equal 3-centimeter sections, with three shaded. The student counted three groups of one fourth inside the shaded amount.

Samira added a verification: three groups of one fourth make three fourths. She also drew a labeled example. The revised instructions took more words but no longer depended on the reader guessing the missing steps.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original fictional instructional narrative, Grade 6 Week 05.

Worked example: The vague phrase “make some pieces” does not tell a reader how many or whether the sections must be equal. “Divide a 12-centimeter bar into four equal 3-centimeter sections” supplies both quantity and relationship.

Student practice
  1. Identify the intended audience, procedure goal and three materials or digital equivalents.
  2. Locate two ambiguous phrases in the first paragraph and cite where they appear.
  3. Explain how the rewritten procedure uses equal parts to represent 3/4 ÷ 1/4 = 3.
  4. Write a four-item checklist a new reader could use to decide whether the directions are complete.

Today's portfolio evidence: Reader analysis, two text citations, materials list and reproducibility checklist.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Let learners use a drawn or described rectangle; no physical craft is required. Test whether the directions can be followed without a teacher demonstration.

Tuesday · Sequence, transitions, and specificity

Learning target: Transform vague commands into a clear series of actions with observable checks.

Expand lesson, worked model and active practice
Academy-original flawed instruction card · Steps That Make You Guess

1. Get some paper and draw a shape.

2. Divide it into parts.

3. Shade a few pieces.

4. Count your answer.

5. Tell someone it is correct.

This card does not specify the whole, the number of equal sections, the shaded portion or a verification. Order words such as first, next, after and finally make instructions easier to follow only when each action itself is precise.

Worked example: First draw a rectangle 12 centimeters long. Next divide it into four equal sections of 3 centimeters. Then shade three sections. Finally count the three one-fourth groups and verify that 3 × 1/4 = 3/4.

Student practice
  1. Rewrite each of the five flawed steps using quantities, units and clear action verbs.
  2. Define partition, equal section, shade, group and verify as technical terms.
  3. Add one warning about unequal parts and one final check using a fraction expression.
  4. Ask a peer or family member to test the text; if none is available, self-test using only your written instructions.

Today's portfolio evidence: An exact five-step procedure, vocabulary notes, and a documented clarity test.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

An independent self-test is equivalent to peer testing. Record one ambiguous instruction before correcting it.

Wednesday · Explain the model and its mathematical meaning

Learning target: Connect procedural steps, explanations and verification using an Academy-original reading.

Expand lesson, worked model and active practice
Academy-original explanatory text · Four One-Sixths

To represent two thirds divided by one sixth, begin with a bar standing for one whole. Divide the bar into six equal sections. Two thirds is equivalent to four sixths, so shade four sections. Each single section is one sixth of the whole. Four one-sixth groups fit within the shaded four-sixths region.

The quotient is 4: 2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4. To verify the result, multiply the number of groups by their size: 4 × 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3. The written check explains why the result matches the diagram, rather than asking a reader to memorize an unrelated operation.

A careful guide identifies the whole and uses equal sections. A different-length rectangle can show the same fraction if all six parts remain equal. A clear caption helps readers understand a diagram even when they cannot see it.

Source: Khaemenes Academy original math-literacy text for Week 05.

Worked example: “Shade four of six equal sections” is a procedure step. “Two thirds equals four sixths” explains why. “4 × 1/6 = 2/3” verifies the model. Distinguish directions, reasons and checks in your annotation.

Student practice
  1. Identify four sequenced directions and one sentence explaining the equivalence.
  2. Write an accessible text caption identifying the whole, six equal sections and four shaded sections.
  3. Paraphrase the verification without changing the underlying fractions.
  4. Explain why unequal segments would make this particular model unreliable.

Today's portfolio evidence: An annotated original explanation, accessible bar caption, and verified paraphrase.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Check whether students explain the reasoning in words. This writing activity does not replace the separate mathematics mastery test.

Thursday · Draft, test and substantively revise

Learning target: Write an original procedure that a new reader can independently reproduce.

Expand lesson, worked model and active practice
Academy-original editor checklist · Can Someone Repeat It?

A procedural editor checks for a clear title, intended audience, materials, exact units, numbered steps and an observable verification. The editor does not assume a reader knows what “a few,” “some,” or “about this much” means. A procedure should describe how to repeat the process, not merely announce the answer.

When a reader finds a missing quantity, the author saves the unclear sentence, records the problem and writes a revision. This before-and-after record shows genuine improvement. Testing a text means following only the written directions instead of silently adding instructions from memory.

Worked example: Original: “Make six parts.” Reader's question: “Of what, and are they equal?” Revised: “Divide the bar representing one whole into six equal sections.” This revision fixes a missing object and relationship.

Student practice
  1. Draft 170–230 original words teaching how to model 2/3 ÷ 1/6, including purpose, materials, numbered directions and verification.
  2. Include a text-only description of your drawing so the guide can be used without specialized supplies.
  3. Record two peer-tester questions or honest self-test failures and revise the relevant steps.
  4. Explain in two sentences what changed and why the final version is easier to follow.

Today's portfolio evidence: A 170–230-word first draft, accessible diagram description and two before/after revisions.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Do not score word count as mastery. Assess whether each step can be followed and whether the verification is meaningful.

Friday · Publish a one-page guide and reflect

Learning target: Evaluate and polish a procedural composition for accuracy, accessibility and reader independence.

Expand lesson, worked model and active practice
Academy-original independent challenge · The Missing Check

An invented instruction card says: “Draw a bar, color four sixths, and write the answer 4.” It never identifies the original division expression, tells the reader what each group represents or explains how to check the quotient. A practiced student may infer the intended activity, but a novice would be missing its purpose and logic.

Use the model texts and your own editor checklist to decide which directions a reader needs, then publish a corrected original guide.

Worked example: A complete conclusion: “Shading 2/3 is the same as shading 4/6 of the whole. Four one-sixth groups fit there. Four groups of 1/6 make 2/3, verifying a quotient of 4.”

Student practice
  1. Identify three missing instructions or explanations in the invented card.
  2. Polish Thursday's guide, saving its original draft and two meaningful revisions.
  3. Verify in writing why 3/4 ÷ 1/4 = 3 and 2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4, using equal-size parts.
  4. Write a three-to-four-sentence reflection about transitions, units and feedback.

Today's portfolio evidence: A one-page procedural guide, revision record, verification and reflection. Teacher-coaching rubric /20: sequence 4, mathematical clarity 5, specificity 5, verification 3, revision 3. Not an official grade.

Teacher / family facilitation guidance

Formal randomized weekly assessment and the 80% mastery gate remain unchanged. Provide feedback on the writing without inventing an official mastery result.

Writer feedback and portfolio evidence are developmental only: no automatic grades or mastery. Existing Week 05 formal assessments and 80% mastery remain authoritative.
