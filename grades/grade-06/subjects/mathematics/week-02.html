Week 02 · Mathematics · Five-Day Learning Studio

Evidence, Annotation, and Ratios

How can a ratio table provide evidence for a fair comparison?

Learning goals and vocabulary

Review Week 1 ratios and unit rates. This week, interpret part-to-part versus part-to-whole ratios; generate and test equivalent ratios; use double number lines and tables; and defend or reject a proportional claim using calculated evidence. Part-to-part compares two groups; part-to-whole compares a group with the total. A proportional relationship keeps a constant multiplier between two quantities.

Monday — Ratios tell different stories

A garden has 6 tomato plants and 4 pepper plants. The tomato-to-pepper ratio is 6:4 = 3:2. The tomato-to-all-plants ratio is 6:10 = 3:5. These represent different questions! Label both categories before writing a ratio. An annotation should explain what each number counts.

Worked example: In a club, 8 students chose chess and 12 chose art. Chess:art = 8:12 = 2:3; chess:total = 8:20 = 2:5. The ratios cannot be swapped because their denominators have different meanings.

Guided questions: (1) If 9 learners walk and 6 cycle, find walk:cycle in simplest form. (2) Find walk:total. (3) Explain which ratio answers “What fraction of all learners walk?”

Tuesday — Build equivalent-ratio tables

A recipe uses 2 cups of oats for every 3 cups of milk. Multiply both quantities by the same factor to preserve taste. A ratio table makes the reasoning visible:

Oats (cups)2468
Milk (cups)36912

Double number line: Mark matching points (0,0), (2,3), (4,6), (6,9). Both lines start at zero, and paired intervals use consistent scaling. For 10 cups oats, 10 ÷ 2 = 5 batches; milk = 3 × 5 = 15 cups.

Practice: (4) Complete 3:5 = 12:__. (5) Fill in milk for 14 cups of oats. (6) Is 8:12 equivalent to 2:3? Prove it using division or multiplication.

Wednesday — Test a claim with evidence

Team A says 4 blue tiles for every 6 red tiles creates the same color ratio as Team B's 10 blue for every 15 red. Claim: They match. Evidence: A = 4:6 = 2:3; B = 10:15 = 2:3. Reasoning: Both ratios reduce to the same pair, so the claim is supported.

Investigation: Compare Plan C (6 cleanups in 9 weeks) and Plan D (10 cleanups in 15 weeks). Write the two rates, simplify them, sketch equivalent pairs, and decide whether the pace is the same. Record what the comparison does not tell you—for example, the quality or duration of each cleanup.

Thursday — Challenge, errors, and revision

  1. A box contains 12 green and 18 yellow beads. Find green:yellow and green:total, both simplified.
  2. Complete 5:8 = 25:__.
  3. A student states 3:4 = 9:16 because both numbers were increased. Explain and correct the mistake.
  4. Decide whether 12:20 and 18:30 are equivalent. Show your proof.
  5. Two drawings use widths and heights of 6 cm × 9 cm and 10 cm × 15 cm. Do they have the same shape proportions? Explain.

Self-review: Mark what each quantity measures; test whether a single multiplier acts on both terms; correct one error with a short explanation.

Optional preview, not a Week 2 mastery prerequisite: The fraction 2/5 is equivalent to 40/100, or 40%. Formal percent instruction follows later in the sequence.

Friday — Independent mastery demonstration

(A) 15 runners and 10 walkers: express runners:walkers and runners:total in simplest form (3 points). (B) Complete a 4:7 ratio for a first quantity of 20; show the scale factor (2 points). (C) Are 9:12 and 15:20 equivalent? Show two steps of evidence (3 points). (D) Explain how a part-to-whole ratio differs from part-to-part (2 points).

Rubric: 10 points; 8/10 meets the 80% mastery standard for teacher review. This lesson does not itself record or unlock verified mastery. Use the Academy's separately authorized assessment and progress records.

Worked answers — review only after trying the activities

1. 3:2; 2. 3:5; 3. walk:total. 4. 20; 5. 21 cups milk; 6. yes, multiply 2:3 by four. Investigation: 6:9 = 2:3 and 10:15 = 2:3, equal rates. 7. 2:3 and 2:5; 8. 40; 9. 3:4 = 9:12, not 9:16; 10. yes, both 3:5; 11. yes, both 2:3. Friday A = 3:2 and 3:5; B = 35, scale factor 5; C = yes, both 3:4; D = distinguish group:group from group:total.

If below mastery: Revisit the two worked examples, draw two labeled models, correct your errors, and take a different authorized mastery check. Do not count a practice worksheet as a scored pass.

Week 02 · Five-Day Mathematical Reasoning Lab

These extra problems deepen the existing ratio lessons. Record a numerical answer, a model, and an explanation for each day. This is formative practice only; it cannot award a verified grade, bypass the formal 80% mastery gate, or change student records.

Monday · Name the comparison

Vocabulary: part-to-part, part-to-whole, simplify, ratio. A mosaic contains 8 amber, 12 blue, and 4 green tiles.

Worked model: Model: amber:blue = 8:12 = 2:3, while amber:all = 8:24 = 1:3. The first ratio compares two colors; the second compares amber to all 24 tiles.

Guided practice: Guided: Write blue:green and simplify. Explain why 12:4 = 3:1.

Independent evidence: Independent: (1) Find green:amber. (2) Find blue:all. (3) A student says amber:blue is 1:3. Diagnose which quantity was confused. (4) Draw a new 24-tile mosaic with the same amber:blue ratio but a different green count.

Teacher review key (open after attempting)

Check: green:amber = 1:2; blue:all = 1:2; amber:blue is 2:3, not 1:3. Student drawings must preserve 2:3 for amber:blue.

Tuesday · Equivalent tables

Vocabulary: equivalent ratio, scale factor, ratio table, multiplicative relationship. Three seedlings require five cups of water.

Worked model: Model: seedlings 3,6,9,12 correspond to water 5,10,15,20. Multiply both quantities by the same positive factor; do not add the same amount to both.

Guided practice: Guided: Complete seedlings 15 → cups __ and 21 → cups __. Explain your multiplier.

Independent evidence: Independent: (1) Find water for 18 seedlings. (2) Find seedlings for 45 cups. (3) Explain why the pair (9,11) does not belong in the table. (4) Produce a different equivalent-ratio table using 4:7.

Teacher review key (open after attempting)

Check: 15→25; 21→35; 18→30; 45→27; (9,11) is incorrect because 9 seedlings require 15 cups. For 4:7, valid rows include (4,7),(8,14),(12,21).

Wednesday · Double number lines

Vocabulary: double number line, correspondence, scaling, proportion. For each 4 meters of ribbon, a project needs 6 buttons.

Worked model: Model: aligned marks at ribbon 0,4,8,12,16 and buttons 0,6,12,18,24 show the same 2:3 ratio. Align zero and corresponding multiples.

Guided practice: Guided: How many buttons for 10 meters? Multiply both values by 2.5: 10 meters corresponds to 15 buttons.

Independent evidence: Independent: (1) Find buttons for 20 meters. (2) Find meters for 33 buttons. (3) Describe why matching 8 meters to 18 buttons is incorrect. (4) Draw a double number line with at least five aligned marks.

Teacher review key (open after attempting)

Check: 20 meters→30 buttons; 33 buttons→22 meters; 8 meters→12 buttons. Validate drawings by matching multiplicative positions, not spacing alone.

Thursday · Challenge a claim

Vocabulary: counterexample, proportional, multiplicative, evidence. Group A uses 12 red and 18 white beads; Group B uses 14 red and 21 white beads.

Worked model: Model: A = 12:18 = 2:3; B = 14:21 = 2:3. The ratios are equivalent even though the bead counts differ.

Guided practice: Guided: Compare 10:15 and 12:20. The first is 2:3 and the second is 3:5, so they are not equivalent.

Independent evidence: Independent: (1) Decide whether 18:24 and 21:28 are equivalent and show both reductions. (2) Critique 'adding 4 to both terms keeps a ratio equivalent.' (3) Compare 9:12 to 15:20. (4) Write one counterexample to the additive claim.

Teacher review key (open after attempting)

Check: 18:24 = 3:4 = 21:28; additive claim false, for instance 2:3 versus 6:7; 9:12 = 3:4 = 15:20.

Friday · Transfer and explanation

Vocabulary: unit, representation, justify, revise. A classroom paints 5 yellow squares for every 8 purple squares.

Worked model: Model: With 40 purple squares, the scale factor is 5, so 25 yellow squares are required. Explain the multiplier as well as the answer.

Guided practice: Guided: For 15 yellow squares, 24 purple squares preserve 5:8. Check by dividing both terms by 3.

Independent evidence: Independent: (1) Find yellow squares for 56 purple. (2) Find purple squares for 35 yellow. (3) Compare yellow:purple with yellow:total for the original pattern. (4) Explain why 10:13 does not describe an equivalent pattern. (5) Design an original ratio context and provide one equivalent and one non-equivalent example.

Teacher review key (open after attempting)

Check: 56 purple→35 yellow; 35 yellow→56 purple; yellow:purple=5:8 while yellow:total=5:13; 10:13 is not equivalent to 5:8. Teacher review required for original contexts and explanations.
