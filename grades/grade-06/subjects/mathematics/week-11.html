Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 Mathematics · Week 11

Statistics: Questions, Data, and Distribution

How can data answer questions while still requiring judgment?

Learning targets

Distinguish statistical from nonstatistical questions; collect and organize variable data; construct dot plots and beginning histograms; compute and interpret mean, median, mode, and range; and critique unsupported claims. Vocabulary: population, sample, variability, frequency, distribution, center, spread, bin, representative sample. All required lesson data are supplied for offline study.

Monday — Ask statistical questions

A statistical question anticipates variability across people or observations. “How many minutes do Grade 6 students read each day?” is statistical because answers vary. “How many minutes did I read yesterday?” asks for one particular value. Define a population, sample, measurement, and clear unit before collecting data.

Practice: identify which of these is statistical: (1) How long do students travel to school? (2) What time did a single bus arrive today? (3) How many books are read by students in one month? Explain why.

Identify variability and statistical questions · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: variability, population, sample. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A question about the heights of all sixth graders expects different measurements; the height of a single named student at noon is a single factual measurement.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Classify 'How many pets does each learner own?' as statistical or not and explain.
  2. Classify 'What is the school's street number?' and justify.
  3. Write a statistical question about travel time, including units.
  4. Identify the population and a possible biased sample in a school lunch survey.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Statistical; nonstatistical; examples ask minutes for all students; population all relevant students, biased sample only students attending a certain lunch club.

Tuesday — Organize data and dot plots

Use the fictional minutes of reading: 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 20, 25, 35. Sort the data, then place one dot per result above a correctly labeled number-line position. Counts: 10→1; 15→2; 20→3; 25→1; 35→1. This makes the most common value visible: 20 minutes.

Practice: draw a dot plot with labeled minutes and a scale of five. Explain why equal horizontal distances must represent equal numeric intervals. Count values at least 20 minutes: five of eight.

Build readable dot plots · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: frequency, scale, distribution. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: For scores 2,2,3,3,3,5 the frequencies are 2→2, 3→3, 5→1. Each observation is one dot above an evenly spaced number line.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Draw a dot plot for 1,1,2,4,4,4,5.
  2. How many readings in that set are at least 4?
  3. Explain why using uneven number-line spacing misleads.
  4. Find the mode and number of observations.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Dots: 1→2, 2→1, 4→3, 5→1; four values ≥4; uneven spacing distorts numerical distance; mode 4, 7 observations.

Wednesday — Center, spread and shape

For the same ordered sample 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 20, 25, 35: sum = 160, mean = 20, median = average of fourth and fifth values = 20, mode = 20, range = 35 − 10 = 25. Mean uses every value; median describes the middle of the ordered distribution. Range provides a basic measure of spread.

Practice: for 2, 4, 4, 6, 9, calculate the mean, median, mode and range. Decide whether an unusually large value could affect the mean more than the median, and explain.

Compare measures of center and spread · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: mean, median, range. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: For 4,5,5,6,20, sum=40 and mean=8, while median=5 and range=16. The unusually large 20 raises the mean.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find mean, median, mode and range of 3,3,5,7,7.
  2. For 2,4,6,8 find the mean and median.
  3. Replace the highest of 4,5,5,6,20 with 10. Find the new mean.
  4. Which center better describes the typical value of 4,5,5,6,20? Defend your choice.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Mean 5, median 5, modes 3 and 7, range 4; mean and median 5; new mean 6; median 5 is less affected by the outlier.

Thursday — Histograms and responsible claims

A histogram groups numeric observations into non-overlapping intervals. For the same reading data, use bins from 10 up to but not including 20, from 20 up to 30, and from 30 up to 40. The frequencies are 3, 4, and 1. Unlike a dot plot, a histogram does not identify every original value. Its bars touch because intervals cover continuous numeric ranges.

Investigate: (1) draw three adjacent histogram bars of heights 3, 4, 1; (2) evaluate the claim “Every student reads 20 minutes” using the dataset; (3) name two ways a non-representative sample could distort a school's conclusion.

Reason about bins and misleading claims · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: histogram, interval, bias. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: For 8,12,15,19,22,25,31, bins [0,10),[10,20),[20,30),[30,40) have frequencies 1,3,2,1.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Count data 5,10,14,20,25,29 in bins [0,10),[10,20),[20,30).
  2. Explain why 20 belongs in the third bin, not the second.
  3. Refute the statement 'Every learner scored 20' when a data set has mean 20.
  4. Identify how sampling only a sports club could bias a school-wide exercise-time survey.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Counts 1,2,3; [10,20) excludes 20; the mean does not show every observation; sports club members may exercise more than typical students.

Friday — Independent evidence

A (2 pts): Write one statistical question and explain why it anticipates variability. B (2 pts): Construct a labeled dot plot of 1, 2, 2, 3, 4. C (3 pts): Find the mean, median, mode and range of 3, 5, 5, 7, 10. D (3 pts): Explain why “most students read exactly the mean” is not justified by the eight-person reading dataset, citing two observations.

Teacher review: 10 points total; 8/10 is the formative 80% benchmark, not a saved verified mastery gate. If mastery is not reached, revise the plot, recalculate statistics, and identify unsupported claims before retaking the formal randomized assessment.

Synthesize statistical evidence · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: claim, evidence, outlier. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: For 6,7,7,8,12 the median is 7, mean 8, range 6. A claim should state which summary it uses.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find mean and median of 2,2,4,6,11.
  2. Construct frequency counts for 1,2,2,3,3,3.
  3. Recall Week 10: solve −2x≥8.
  4. Recall Week 04: find 30% of 90.
  5. Explain why a convenience sample may not represent all students.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Mean 5 and median 4; 1→1, 2→2, 3→3; x≤−4; 27; selection bias limits inference.
