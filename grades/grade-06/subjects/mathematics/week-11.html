Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 Mathematics · Week 11

Statistics: Questions, Data, and Distribution

How can data answer questions while still requiring judgment?

Learning targets

Distinguish statistical from nonstatistical questions; collect and organize variable data; construct dot plots and beginning histograms; compute and interpret mean, median, mode, and range; and critique unsupported claims. Vocabulary: population, sample, variability, frequency, distribution, center, spread, bin, representative sample. All required lesson data are supplied for offline study.

Monday — Ask statistical questions

A statistical question anticipates variability across people or observations. “How many minutes do Grade 6 students read each day?” is statistical because answers vary. “How many minutes did I read yesterday?” asks for one particular value. Define a population, sample, measurement, and clear unit before collecting data.

Practice: identify which of these is statistical: (1) How long do students travel to school? (2) What time did a single bus arrive today? (3) How many books are read by students in one month? Explain why.

Tuesday — Organize data and dot plots

Use the fictional minutes of reading: 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 20, 25, 35. Sort the data, then place one dot per result above a correctly labeled number-line position. Counts: 10→1; 15→2; 20→3; 25→1; 35→1. This makes the most common value visible: 20 minutes.

Practice: draw a dot plot with labeled minutes and a scale of five. Explain why equal horizontal distances must represent equal numeric intervals. Count values at least 20 minutes: five of eight.

Wednesday — Center, spread and shape

For the same ordered sample 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 20, 25, 35: sum = 160, mean = 20, median = average of fourth and fifth values = 20, mode = 20, range = 35 − 10 = 25. Mean uses every value; median describes the middle of the ordered distribution. Range provides a basic measure of spread.

Practice: for 2, 4, 4, 6, 9, calculate the mean, median, mode and range. Decide whether an unusually large value could affect the mean more than the median, and explain.

Thursday — Histograms and responsible claims

A histogram groups numeric observations into non-overlapping intervals. For the same reading data, use bins from 10 up to but not including 20, from 20 up to 30, and from 30 up to 40. The frequencies are 3, 4, and 1. Unlike a dot plot, a histogram does not identify every original value. Its bars touch because intervals cover continuous numeric ranges.

Investigate: (1) draw three adjacent histogram bars of heights 3, 4, 1; (2) evaluate the claim “Every student reads 20 minutes” using the dataset; (3) name two ways a non-representative sample could distort a school's conclusion.

Friday — Independent evidence

A (2 pts): Write one statistical question and explain why it anticipates variability. B (2 pts): Construct a labeled dot plot of 1, 2, 2, 3, 4. C (3 pts): Find the mean, median, mode and range of 3, 5, 5, 7, 10. D (3 pts): Explain why “most students read exactly the mean” is not justified by the eight-person reading dataset, citing two observations.

Teacher review: 10 points total; 8/10 is the formative 80% benchmark, not a saved verified mastery gate. If mastery is not reached, revise the plot, recalculate statistics, and identify unsupported claims before retaking the formal randomized assessment.

Answer key and correction guide

Review after completing the exercises

Monday: questions 1 and 3 are statistical; question 2 records one specific result. Tuesday: dot heights 1, 2, 3, 1, 1 at 10, 15, 20, 25, 35; 5/8 are at least 20 minutes. Wednesday: 2,4,4,6,9 has mean 5, median 4, mode 4, range 7. Thursday: histogram 3,4,1; five of eight values are not exactly 20, so “every student” is false. Friday C: 3,5,5,7,10 has mean 6, median 5, mode 5, range 7; D: only three of eight read exactly 20 while values range from 10 to 35.
