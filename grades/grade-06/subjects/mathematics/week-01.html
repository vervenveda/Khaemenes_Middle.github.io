Week 01 · Mathematics · Five-Day Learning Studio

Ratios and Unit Rates: Scholar Launch

Essential question: How can fair comparisons help us plan time, resources, and responsible choices?

Crosswalk diagnostic — Number sense and explanation

Before Monday: Solve these independently to document readiness: 48 ÷ 6; 3/4 + 1/4; compare 0.6 and 2/3; show 15% of 80; and explain why 2 × (3 + 4) equals 14. Record methods, not just answers. These are practice questions, not the verified weekly examination.

Reasoning check: Interpret 3:5 as a part-to-part comparison. Explain how it differs from three out of eight total parts. Use this to bridge the diagnostic into the existing ratio lessons.

Correct and reflect: Check answers only after trying: 8; 1; 0.6 < 2/3; 12; distributive or grouped operations yield 14. Identify one error pattern and write a corrected explanation. Teacher/parent review is optional; do not alter saved mastery based on this diagnostic.

Learning targets and prerequisites

By Friday, you will write and simplify ratios, construct equivalent-ratio tables, calculate unit rates with units, compare two offers fairly, and explain your reasoning. You need multiplication, division and fraction basics. All instruction and practice below work offline.

Vocabulary: A ratio compares quantities by division; an equivalent ratio expresses the same comparison after multiplying or dividing both terms by the same nonzero number; a rate compares quantities with different units; a unit rate expresses the rate per one unit.

Monday — Learn to compare quantities

Imagine a classroom has 12 notebooks and 8 folders. The ratio of notebooks to folders is 12:8, read “12 to 8.” Divide both numbers by 4 to simplify: 3:2. This says there are three notebooks for every two folders. Reversing the order gives 8:12, which compares folders to notebooks. Order matters.

Worked example A: A recipe uses 6 cups water for 4 cups concentrate. Water:concentrate = 6:4 = 3:2. To make the same mixture with 9 cups water, multiply each part of 3:2 by 3: 9:6.

Tuesday — Rates and unit rates

Ratios can compare unlike units. A cyclist travels 48 miles in 4 hours. Divide distance by time: 48 miles ÷ 4 hours = 12 miles per hour. “Per” means “for each one.” The unit rate is 12 mph. Always write the units; 12 alone does not communicate the comparison.

Worked example B: Six notebooks cost $9. The cost for one notebook is $9 ÷ 6 = $1.50 per notebook. To estimate the cost of ten notebooks at the same rate: 10 × $1.50 = $15.

Common mistake: $9 ÷ 6 is dollars per notebook; 6 ÷ $9 measures notebooks per dollar. Both can be meaningful, but they answer different questions.

Guided practice: (4) A car travels 180 miles in 3 hours: find mph. (5) Eight markers cost $12: find dollars per marker. (6) Fourteen pages in 2 minutes: find pages per minute. (7) Explain the difference between 5 dollars per book and 5 books per dollar.

Wednesday — Investigation: a fair supply purchase

Two offers supply the same type of pencil: Pack A has 12 pencils for $3.60; Pack B has 20 pencils for $5.40. Find the unit price of each before making a recommendation.

  1. Record quantity, cost, and units for each pack.
  2. Calculate price per pencil to the nearest cent: $3.60 ÷ 12 = $0.30; $5.40 ÷ 20 = $0.27.
  3. Use a table or clearly labeled drawing to show what 60 pencils would cost at each unit rate.
  4. Recommend one offer, cite both unit prices, and explain whether budget, waste, or needed quantity might affect the decision.

Investigation notebook: Include your question, calculation, labeled model, conclusion and a limitation (for example, a pack-size restriction). A lower price per pencil does not automatically mean every buyer should purchase the larger pack.

Thursday — Independent application and revision

Solve without looking at worked examples first. Show your strategy and units.

  1. Simplify 18:24.
  2. Complete the equivalent ratio: 4:7 = 16:__.
  3. A water bottle holds 750 mL. How many mL per 1 of 3 equal servings?
  4. A train travels 225 km in 5 hours. Find km per hour.
  5. Store C sells 15 glue sticks for $6.75; Store D sells 10 for $4.80. Which has the lower price per stick, and by how much?
  6. Two students claim 2:3 and 6:9 are not equivalent because all four numbers differ. Correct their reasoning with a model or equation.

Self-check: Check that both terms of an equivalent ratio use the same multiplier, that the division order matches the requested units, and that the conclusion answers the actual question. Correct at least one error, or explain a check that confirmed your work.

Friday — Evidence and mastery

Independent demonstration: (A) Simplify 21:28 and show why. (B) A runner completes 30 km in 3 hours: calculate the unit rate. (C) Compare 9 pens for $4.05 with 12 pens for $5.16, showing both unit prices. (D) Explain in two or more sentences why units and ratio order matter.

Rubric (10 points): A = 2 points (ratio and explanation); B = 2 (calculation and units); C = 4 (two correct rates, comparison, reasoning); D = 2 (accurate conceptual explanation). 8/10 is 80% mastery.
