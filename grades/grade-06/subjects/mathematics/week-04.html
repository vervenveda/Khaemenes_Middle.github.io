Week 04 · Mathematics · Five-Day Learning Studio

Percent, Proportion, and Civic Decisions

How can percentages help a community make informed decisions?

Learning targets

Convert fractions, decimals, and percents; find the part, whole, and percent; interpret data displays; and defend responsible conclusions from surveys and budgets. Vocabulary: percent, proportion, part, whole, sample, percentage point, relative change. All examples and activities are available offline.

Monday — Understand percent

Percent means “per 100.” 25% = 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25. In a 100-square grid, shading 25 squares shows 25%. To convert 0.6 to percent, multiply by 100: 60%. To convert 15% to a decimal, divide by 100: 0.15.

Worked example: 40% of 80 = 0.40 × 80 = 32. Estimate first: 40% is less than half, so 32 is reasonable.

Practice: (1) Convert 3/4 to a percent. (2) Write 0.08 as a percent. (3) Find 20% of 65.

Tuesday — Percent, part, and whole

The relationship is part = (percent ÷ 100) × whole. To find the percent, calculate (part ÷ whole) × 100. If 18 of 60 students choose walking, 18 ÷ 60 = 0.3 = 30%. To find the whole when 12 represents 20%, compute 12 ÷ 0.2 = 60.

Worked example: A survey of 50 participants has 35 supporting a proposed garden. 35/50 = 70%. Never say “70 people” when the evidence says “70 percent.”

Practice: (4) What percent is 9 of 36? (5) If 15 is 25% of a group, how many are in the group? (6) Find 15% of $240.

Wednesday — Community survey evidence

Original classroom case study (fictional): 80 residents respond to a survey about a shared community space. Of those surveyed, 48 choose a garden, 20 choose a reading room, and 12 choose a sports area. Verify the total is 80. Calculate 48/80 = 60%, 20/80 = 25%, and 12/80 = 15%. Percentages total 100%.

Create a labeled bar chart from the data, using a vertical axis from 0% to 100%. Then write a claim supported by at least two percentages. Discuss the limitation: these 80 respondents might not represent all residents, and preferences are not a binding public vote.

Investigation: (7) If only 40 people responded, would 60% still necessarily represent 48 people? (8) Describe a way to collect more representative survey evidence.

Thursday — Compare claims and budgets

Suppose a community committee has a hypothetical $1,200 budget. An outreach plan uses 25%, supplies use 40%, and the remaining money is reserved. Outreach = $300, supplies = $480, and reserve = $420 (35%). Check that 300 + 480 + 420 = 1,200.

Worked comparison: Proposal A serves 36 out of 60 families (60%); Proposal B serves 45 out of 90 (50%). B serves more families in absolute number; A serves a greater share. A responsible conclusion states which measure it uses.

Independent practice: (9) Find 35% of $800. (10) What percent is 27 out of 45? (11) A 10% increase on $50 gives what new value? (12) Explain the difference between 10 percentage points and a 10% relative increase.

Friday — Independent mastery demonstration

A (2 points): Convert 3/5 into a decimal and percent. B (2 points): Find 15% of $200, showing the calculation. C (3 points): In a 120-person survey, 72 prefer option A, 30 prefer B, and 18 prefer C; calculate each share and confirm the total is 100%. D (3 points): Write a fair, evidence-based recommendation using the survey, including one limitation of the sample.

The teacher-review rubric totals 10 points; 8/10 meets the 80% instructional standard. This written activity does not itself award verified progression or replace the authorized Academy assessment.

If below 80%, revisit a relevant worked example, correct the calculation and its explanation, and complete a new authorized mastery check.

Answer explanations — check after completing the practice

Reveal answers and reasoning

1. 75%; 2. 8%; 3. 13; 4. 25%; 5. 60; 6. $36; 7. No: 60% of 40 is 24, not 48; 8. Recruit respondents from different neighborhoods and groups using a clear selection method; 9. $280; 10. 60%; 11. $55; 12. A rise from 20% to 30% is 10 percentage points but a 50% relative increase. Friday A 0.6 and 60%; B $30; C 60%, 25%, 15%; D should distinguish respondents from the entire population and cite data accurately.
