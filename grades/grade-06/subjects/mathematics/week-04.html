Week 04 · Mathematics · Five-Day Learning Studio

Percent, Proportion, and Civic Decisions

How can percentages help a community make informed decisions?

Learning targets

Convert fractions, decimals, and percents; find the part, whole, and percent; interpret data displays; and defend responsible conclusions from surveys and budgets. Vocabulary: percent, proportion, part, whole, sample, percentage point, relative change. All examples and activities are available offline.

Monday — Understand percent

Percent means “per 100.” 25% = 25/100 = 1/4 = 0.25. In a 100-square grid, shading 25 squares shows 25%. To convert 0.6 to percent, multiply by 100: 60%. To convert 15% to a decimal, divide by 100: 0.15.

Worked example: 40% of 80 = 0.40 × 80 = 32. Estimate first: 40% is less than half, so 32 is reasonable.

Practice: (1) Convert 3/4 to a percent. (2) Write 0.08 as a percent. (3) Find 20% of 65.

Visualize and convert percent · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: percent, hundredths, fraction, decimal, benchmark. Define two terms and use one in a mathematically accurate sentence.

Worked model: A 10 by 10 grid has 35 shaded squares. That is 35/100 = 7/20 = 0.35 = 35%. A full grid of 100 squares helps students see that 0.35 is less than one half.

Guided practice: Draw a 100-square model and shade 62 squares. State 62/100, 0.62, and 62%.

Misconception clinic: A percent means parts per hundred; 0.9 is 90%, whereas 0.9% is 0.009.

  1. Express 7/20 as a decimal and a percent.
  2. Convert 0.045 into a percent; explain why it is less than 5%.
  3. Find 35% of 60 students and explain why 35 is not the student count.
  4. Correct the claim that 0.9% = 0.9 as a decimal.
Teacher answer key — open after your attempt

1. 0.35 = 35%. 2. 4.5%, less than 5%. 3. 21 students. 4. 0.9% = 0.009, not 0.9.

Tuesday — Percent, part, and whole

The relationship is part = (percent ÷ 100) × whole. To find the percent, calculate (part ÷ whole) × 100. If 18 of 60 students choose walking, 18 ÷ 60 = 0.3 = 30%. To find the whole when 12 represents 20%, compute 12 ÷ 0.2 = 60.

Worked example: A survey of 50 participants has 35 supporting a proposed garden. 35/50 = 70%. Never say “70 people” when the evidence says “70 percent.”

Practice: (4) What percent is 9 of 36? (5) If 15 is 25% of a group, how many are in the group? (6) Find 15% of $240.

Find an unknown part, whole, or percent · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: part, whole, base, rate, inverse operation. Define two terms and use one in a mathematically accurate sentence.

Worked model: A museum has 36 student visitors, which are 30% of its total visitors. Represent this with 36 = 0.30 × whole. Divide: whole = 36 ÷ 0.30 = 120 visitors. Check: 30% of 120 is 36.

Guided practice: In a club, 15 members are 25% of all members; 15 ÷ 0.25 = 60. Verify with a tape diagram split into four equal sections.

Misconception clinic: To find the whole from a part and a percent, divide the part by the percent written as a decimal.

  1. Find 18% of 250, explaining how 10%, 5%, and 3% can be combined.
  2. 24 is what percent of 80? Express the answer in two forms.
  3. A group of 21 learners is 35% of all learners. Find the whole.
  4. A student calculates 25 ÷ 0.5 = 12.5. Identify and fix the error.
Teacher answer key — open after your attempt

1. 45. 2. 24/80 = 0.30 = 30%. 3. 21 ÷ 0.35 = 60. 4. 25 ÷ 0.5 = 50, which can be checked because half of 50 is 25.

Wednesday — Community survey evidence

Original classroom case study (fictional): 80 residents respond to a survey about a shared community space. Of those surveyed, 48 choose a garden, 20 choose a reading room, and 12 choose a sports area. Verify the total is 80. Calculate 48/80 = 60%, 20/80 = 25%, and 12/80 = 15%. Percentages total 100%.

Create a labeled bar chart from the data, using a vertical axis from 0% to 100%. Then write a claim supported by at least two percentages. Discuss the limitation: these 80 respondents might not represent all residents, and preferences are not a binding public vote.

Investigation: (7) If only 40 people responded, would 60% still necessarily represent 48 people? (8) Describe a way to collect more representative survey evidence.

Investigate survey evidence critically · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: sample, population, representation, denominator, bias. Define two terms and use one in a mathematically accurate sentence.

Worked model: Two groups were surveyed: 30 of 50 in Area A favor a garden (60%), while 42 of 70 in Area B favor a garden (60%). Equal percentages can describe different counts. Combined support is 72 of 120, also 60%; this equality is specific to these figures.

Guided practice: A survey of 40 people records 22 supporters. The sample support is 55%. Explain why it does not prove that 55% of the entire community supports the plan.

Misconception clinic: Survey percentages describe the observed sample unless sampling supports generalizing to a wider population.

  1. Compare 18 of 30 respondents and 24 of 40 respondents. Calculate both percentages.
  2. A poll has 48 supporters among 80 responses. If only half as many people responded with the same percentage, how many supporters would there be?
  3. A neighborhood poll includes only people attending a park meeting. Identify a possible source of sample bias.
  4. Write one cautious recommendation from a fictional poll of 60 respondents: 33 support a reading room, 18 a garden, and 9 a sports court.
Teacher answer key — open after your attempt

1. Both 60%. 2. 24 supporters among 40 respondents. 3. The attendees may not represent all neighborhood residents. 4. The sample shares are 55%, 30%, and 15%; any recommendation must refer to respondents, not automatically to the entire community.

Thursday — Compare claims and budgets

Suppose a community committee has a hypothetical $1,200 budget. An outreach plan uses 25%, supplies use 40%, and the remaining money is reserved. Outreach = $300, supplies = $480, and reserve = $420 (35%). Check that 300 + 480 + 420 = 1,200.

Worked comparison: Proposal A serves 36 out of 60 families (60%); Proposal B serves 45 out of 90 (50%). B serves more families in absolute number; A serves a greater share. A responsible conclusion states which measure it uses.

Independent practice: (9) Find 35% of $800. (10) What percent is 27 out of 45? (11) A 10% increase on $50 gives what new value? (12) Explain the difference between 10 percentage points and a 10% relative increase.

Make responsible budget comparisons · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: allocation, remainder, percentage point, relative change, estimate. Define two terms and use one in a mathematically accurate sentence.

Worked model: A $1,600 budget assigns 30% to supplies and 45% to events. Supplies receive $480, events receive $720, and the remaining 25% is $400. The total $480 + $720 + $400 = $1,600 verifies the calculation.

Guided practice: A fee increases from $40 to $50. The increase is $10; relative to the original $40 it is 10/40 = 25%. The difference is not 10 percentage points because these are money amounts, not percentages.

Misconception clinic: A percentage-point change differs from a relative percent change; successive percentages often act on different bases.

  1. Allocate 15% of a $2,400 project budget to printing and 35% to supplies. Find the remaining dollars.
  2. A survey's support rate rises from 40% to 50%. Give its change in percentage points and its relative percent increase.
  3. A product costs $80, receives a 25% discount, then 10% sales tax on its discounted price. Find its final price.
  4. Compare a proposed $54 fee with an old $45 fee; calculate the relative percent increase.
Teacher answer key — open after your attempt

1. $360 to printing, $840 to supplies, $1,200 remaining (50%). 2. 10 percentage points; relative increase 10/40 = 25%. 3. Discounted $60, tax $6, final $66. 4. ($54−$45)/$45 = 20% increase.

Friday — Independent mastery demonstration

Demonstrate transfer and cumulative retention · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: justify, proportion, evidence, evaluate, revise. Define two terms and use one in a mathematically accurate sentence.

Worked model: For 84 supporters among 140 respondents, divide 84/140 = 0.6 = 60%. A claim about the sample may be justified, but a claim about every resident requires a defensible sampling process.

Guided practice: Revisit a Week 03 rate: 90 units in 6 hours = 15 units/hour. Explain why a unit rate and a percent answer different comparison questions.

Misconception clinic: Mathematical answers must make sense in context; a percentage applied to people can reveal rounding or imperfect data.

  1. Convert 9/25 into a percent, showing an equivalent fraction with denominator 100.
  2. In a group of 240, 18% volunteer. Determine the number of volunteers; explain the meaning of a fractional result if a context produces one.
  3. A $900 budget allocates 20%, 30%, and the remaining amount. Calculate all three dollar amounts and check their sum.
  4. A sample includes 75 participants; 45 favor a choice. Calculate the percentage and identify one limitation of making a population-wide claim.
  5. A vehicle travels 135 miles in 3 hours. Compare that unit rate with Week 03 reasoning, then explain why it cannot be expressed as a percent without specifying a reference value.
Teacher answer key — open after your attempt

1. 9/25 = 36/100 = 36%. 2. 43.2, revealing that 18% of 240 is not a whole count; if the original percent was rounded, require the exact observed count rather than treating 43.2 people as real. 3. $180, $270, $450; sum $900. 4. 45/75 = 60%; sample selection and size affect generalizability. 5. 45 miles/hour; a percent requires a stated whole or baseline.
