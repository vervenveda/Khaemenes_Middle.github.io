Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 Mathematics · Week 20

Cells, Systems, and Scientific Models: Percentages

How do percentages help describe observations of small structures?

Learning goals

Convert fractions to percentages, determine a percent of a whole, compare samples fairly, estimate with proportional reasoning, and acknowledge uncertainty. All sample numbers are hypothetical and suitable for offline study.

Monday — Understand percentages

In a fictional microscope image with 80 marked cells, 20 are classified in a particular category. The share is 20/80 = 1/4 = 25%. The percent describes the observed image, not necessarily an entire organism.

Practice: express 12 of 60 and 18 of 90 as percents; identify denominators.

Interpret sample percentages · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: fraction, percent, denominator. Define these terms and use at least one in your mathematical explanation.

Worked model: Of 72 fictional cell-image marks, 18 are classified as Type A: 18/72=1/4=25%. This statement describes only the sampled marks.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Convert 14 of 70 image marks into a percentage.
  2. Find the percentage of 27 marked cells among 90.
  3. A student reports 30 of 60 as 30%. Diagnose the error.
  4. Explain why 25% in one image cannot establish the proportion in every image.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

20%; 30%; 30/60=50%; one image may be unrepresentative of a larger population.

Tuesday — Percent of a whole

For a model population of 150 cells, 40% is 0.40 × 150 = 60 cells. In reverse, if 45 of 180 cells have a feature, the percentage is 45 ÷ 180 × 100 = 25%.

Practice: find 30% of 120; determine the percent represented by 21 of 70.

Compute part and whole · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: part, whole, rate, estimate. Define these terms and use at least one in your mathematical explanation.

Worked model: If 35% of 240 fictional observations meet a category, the modeled count is 0.35×240=84. Verify 84/240=0.35.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find 15% of 180.
  2. If 28 of 80 image regions are marked, find percent.
  3. If 45 corresponds to 30% of a sample, find total sample size.
  4. A rounded share yields 43.2 hypothetical cells. Explain why a real counted-cell total must be a whole number.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

27; 35%; 150; a fractional count flags rounding or use of an approximate percentage, not 0.2 of a counted cell.

Wednesday — Compare sample percentages

Sample A has 18 of 60 marked objects (30%). Sample B has 24 of 80 (30%). Raw counts differ but sample proportions agree. Use the same category definition and a meaningful denominator.

Practice: compare 15 of 50 to 20 of 80; write a conclusion with calculations.

Compare shares fairly · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: sample, proportion, reference group. Define these terms and use at least one in your mathematical explanation.

Worked model: Sample A contains 16 of 40 marked regions (40%); sample B has 27 of 90 (30%). B has more marked regions in total, but A has the higher proportion.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Compare 18/45 with 30/75.
  2. Compare 32/80 with 21/60.
  3. Explain why comparing the numerators alone is misleading.
  4. Write a cautious conclusion about two fictional sample percentages.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Both 40%; 40% versus 35%, first higher; denominators differ; conclusions should describe these samples rather than all real organisms.

Thursday — Estimate and address uncertainty

A sample of 40 image regions yields 10 marked. The observed proportion is 25%. Predicting 25 marked in a new set of 100 is a proportional estimate, not a guarantee: sampling methods and image classification can influence the result.

Practice: make a model-based prediction for 200 regions; state one limitation of extrapolation.

Model predictions with uncertainty · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: extrapolate, uncertainty, representation. Define these terms and use at least one in your mathematical explanation.

Worked model: If 12 of 48 sampled regions are marked (25%), a proportional estimate for 200 regions is 50, assuming the same rate; actual counts can differ.

Independent reasoning:

  1. If 24 of 80 are marked, predict marks among 150 at the same rate.
  2. A sample has 9/30 marked; predict a count for 90 using a constant proportion.
  3. Identify two reasons the predicted number might differ from observation.
  4. Recall Week 19: calculate an image enlargement factor from 4 cm to 12 cm.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

45; 27; sampling variation, changing classification or nonrepresentative selection; factor 3.

Friday — Formative review

A (2 points): express 9/36 as a percent. B (2 points): find 35% of 80. C (3 points): compare 12/40 against 21/70. D (3 points): explain why a 25% rate observed in one simulated image does not automatically prove a population-wide rate.

Synthesize percent and scale reasoning · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: justify, conversion, limitation. Define these terms and use at least one in your mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A diagram at scale 2,000× shows 60 mm for an object modeled as 0.03 mm; 60÷0.03=2,000. Units must match before dividing.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Convert 12/48 to a percent.
  2. Find 28% of 150; discuss whether a counted result must be whole.
  3. If a 40 µm object is drawn as 8 cm, compute enlargement after converting 8 cm to 80,000 µm.
  4. Recall Week 18: rate for 45 pulses in 9 seconds.
  5. Identify one limitation of drawing conclusions from hypothetical images.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

25%; 42; 80,000/40=2,000×; 5 pulses/s; simulated data do not independently establish real biological claims.

Crosswalk studio — Cell diagrams, scale and measurement

Monday bridge: A fictional cell drawing 8 cm across represents an object with modeled diameter 40 micrometers. The illustration uses 80 mm ÷ 0.04 mm = 2,000× enlargement; a model label must identify both units.

Tuesday bridge: If 1 cm on a fictional diagram represents 5 µm, then a 3 cm drawing represents 15 µm. A scale bar expresses a modeled relation; it is not independent microscopy evidence.

Wednesday bridge: Compare two fictional diagrams of 4 cm and 6 cm representing the same 20 µm object; their enlargement factors are 2,000× and 3,000×, not different actual cell sizes.

Thursday bridge: A schematic of 80 cells with 20 marked gives 20/80=25%. A separate fictional sample of 40 with 12 marked gives 30%. Different proportions alone do not prove differences between real tissues.

Friday evidence: Label a fictional cell illustration, convert µm to mm, calculate an enlargement, draw a scale bar and compare two proportions with an evidence-limit sentence. Check: 2,000×; 15 µm; 2,000× versus 3,000×; 25% versus 30%.

Answer key

Open after completing practice

Monday: both 20%. Tuesday: 36 and 30%. Wednesday: 30% versus 25%. Thursday: estimate 50 in 200, subject to sampling assumptions. Friday: 25%; 28; both 30%; sample estimates do not establish population truths.
