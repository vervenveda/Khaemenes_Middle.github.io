Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 Mathematics · Week 23

Weather, Climate, and Human Patterns: Coordinate Planes

How do coordinates help describe and evaluate spatial patterns?

Learning goals

Plot signed coordinates in all four quadrants, label axes and units, calculate horizontal and vertical distances, perform reflections, and distinguish location from measurements. All stations and maps are fictional models.

Monday — Coordinate axes and quadrants

A coordinate pair (x,y) names a location on a model grid. The first coordinate is horizontal (east/right positive); the second is vertical (north/up positive). Point (3,−2) lies in Quadrant IV, while (−4,2) lies in Quadrant II. Coordinates describe position on a chosen grid, not actual geographic longitude and latitude.

Practice: plot (2,3), (−3,4), (−2,−1), (4,−3) and identify quadrants.

Read coordinates and signs · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: ordered pair, quadrant, axis. Define the terms and use one in a clear mathematical statement.

Worked model: On a fictional map, (−5,3) is five grid units left and three up from the origin. It lies in Quadrant II; the coordinates describe a position, not a temperature.

Guided pathway: Start at the origin. Move horizontally for x, vertically for y, then classify the signs. Check that axis points are not assigned to quadrants.

Misconception to avoid: Swapping x and y changes the location, even if both numbers remain the same.

Self-check before the teacher key: Plot (3,5), (−4,−2), (6,−1), (−3,7) and check that all four quadrant labels are represented.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Identify quadrants of (3,5), (−4,−2), (6,−1), and (−3,7).
  2. Name the coordinates of a point five left and four down.
  3. Why is (2,−5) different from (−5,2)?
  4. Is (0,6) in a quadrant? Explain.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

I, III, IV, II; (−5,−4); order assigns horizontal then vertical distance; no, it lies on the y-axis.

Tuesday — Map simulated stations

A fictional weather map positions Station A at (−4,3), B at (2,3), and C at (2,−2) on a square grid measured in kilometers. A to B is 6 units horizontally. B to C is 5 units vertically. Axis orientation and units must be stated to interpret distances.

Practice: find horizontal distance from (−5,1) to (3,1), and vertical distance from (2,−4) to (2,5).

Measure horizontal and vertical routes · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: grid scale, distance, horizontal. Define the terms and use one in a clear mathematical statement.

Worked model: With one grid unit representing 2 km, the horizontal distance from (−4,2) to (3,2) is 7 units = 14 km; this does not establish real travel time.

Guided pathway: Check whether x or y stays constant. Subtract the changing coordinates, take absolute distance, then apply the stated scale only when provided.

Misconception to avoid: Subtracting coordinate values without considering signs may undercount distance across the origin.

Self-check before the teacher key: From x=−5 to x=1 is six grid intervals; six times 3 km per interval equals 18 km.

Independent reasoning:

  1. At 1 unit = 3 km, find the distance from (−5,1) to (1,1).
  2. Find vertical distance from (2,−6) to (2,3) in grid units.
  3. Find a horizontal and vertical route from A(−2,−1) to B(3,4).
  4. Why can grid distance differ from actual road distance?
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

18 km; 9 units; move right 5 and up 5; routes, obstacles and road shapes differ from a straight grid path.

Wednesday — Reflections and patterns

Reflecting (−3,2) across the y-axis gives (3,2); reflecting it across the x-axis gives (−3,−2). The sign of only the coordinate perpendicular to the mirror axis changes. These operations can model symmetries in a diagram without implying symmetry in actual weather.

Practice: reflect (4,−5) across each axis. Explain why (−4,5) reflects across both axes, not just one.

Use reflections to test symmetry · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: reflection, x-axis, y-axis. Define the terms and use one in a clear mathematical statement.

Worked model: Reflecting (−4,5) over the x-axis gives (−4,−5); over the y-axis gives (4,5). Across both axes it becomes (4,−5).

Guided pathway: Sketch the mirror axis before changing signs: over x-axis change y; over y-axis change x. For two reflections, check both signs.

Misconception to avoid: Reflecting across the x-axis does not reverse the x-coordinate.

Self-check before the teacher key: Apply a second reflection to your result; reflecting twice across the same axis must return the original point.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Reflect (6,−2) across the x-axis.
  2. Reflect (−7,3) across the y-axis.
  3. Reflect (−2,−4) across both axes.
  4. Explain how reflecting (0,5) over the y-axis changes the point.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

(6,2); (7,3); (2,4); it remains (0,5) because it lies on the reflection axis.

Thursday — Compare data claims

Fictional stations at (−2,4), (0,4), and (3,4) are plotted along a common horizontal line. That geometry does not imply equal rainfall or temperature. For a meaningful graph, distinguish spatial position from measured variables and cite a separate data table.

Practice: design a grid with three positions and separate temperature labels; identify one limitation of a small station network.

Separate place data from measurement data · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: variable, evidence, location. Define the terms and use one in a clear mathematical statement.

Worked model: Three modeled stations at (−3,2), (1,2), and (4,2) have the same y-coordinate; nothing about this alone establishes equal rainfall.

Guided pathway: Separate location coordinates from measured weather quantities. Compare the temperature numbers using subtraction, then check the grid scale separately.

Misconception to avoid: Stations sharing a y-coordinate do not necessarily share weather observations or an identical time of measurement.

Self-check before the teacher key: For 12°C and 8°C the difference is 4°C; from x=−2 to x=3 there are five grid intervals.

Independent reasoning:

  1. List two measurements you would need to compare actual weather at stations.
  2. A map labels A(−2,4) at 12°C and B(3,4) at 8°C. What is the temperature difference?
  3. At 1 km/grid unit, find A-to-B horizontal separation.
  4. Explain why two points on the same horizontal line may differ in temperature.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

Temperature/time and rainfall or another relevant observation; 4°C; 5 km; spatial location is not the same as a weather measurement.

Integrate coordinates and prior skills · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: scale, explanation, verification. Define the terms and use one in a clear mathematical statement.

Worked model: On a grid scaled 1 unit = 4 km, two points at (−1,2) and (3,2) are 4 grid units or 16 km apart.

Guided pathway: Identify whether each task needs a reflection, scaled distance, signed fraction or percent. State one uncertainty before making a model-based claim.

Misconception to avoid: A made-up coordinate map cannot certify actual geography, timing or local conditions.

Self-check before the teacher key: Recalculate the distance using seven intervals at 2 km per interval and confirm 14 km.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Reflect (−4,−3) across the y-axis.
  2. Find distance from (−5,4) to (2,4) if each unit is 2 km.
  3. Recall Week 22: evaluate −3/4 + 1/2.
  4. Recall Week 20: find 18 of 60 as a percentage.
  5. Explain an uncertainty in a simulated station map.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

(4,−3); 14 km; −1/4; 30%; simulated position and sampling conditions cannot establish real-world geography or weather.
