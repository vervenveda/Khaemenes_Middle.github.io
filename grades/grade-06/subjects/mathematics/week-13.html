Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 · Mathematics · Week 13

Volume and Three-Dimensional Modeling

How can 3D models solve real problems?

Learning goals

Model and calculate rectangular-prism volume using unit cubes, whole and fractional edge lengths, unknown dimensions, composite figures, and real design constraints. Always distinguish cubic volume units from square surface-area units.

Monday — Cubic units and volume

A solid occupies volume measured in cubic units, unlike surface area, which measures its outside faces. A rectangular prism with length 5 cm, width 4 cm, and height 3 cm contains 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 cubic centimeters. Imagine 3 layers of 20 unit cubes.

Practice: calculate volumes of prisms measuring 6 × 2 × 4 cm and 8 × 3 × 2 m. Explain why cm³, not cm², is correct.

Model unit-cube layers · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: cubic unit, layer, volume. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A 5×3×4 prism has 15 cubes per layer and four layers, so 60 cubic units.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find volume of 7×2×3 cm prism.
  2. How many unit cubes are in a 4×4×5 prism?
  3. Why does a unit cube have three unit-length dimensions?
  4. Distinguish 12 cm² from 12 cm³ in a model.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

42 cm³; 80; cubic units measure 3D space; cm² covers a face while cm³ fills volume.

Tuesday — Fractional edge lengths

For rectangular prisms V = length × width × height even with fractional dimensions. A prism measuring 3/2 cm × 2 cm × 4 cm has volume 12 cm³. Check by multiplying 1.5 × 2 × 4. Fractions describe lengths; the product describes three-dimensional space.

Practice: volume for 1/2 m × 4 m × 3 m; and 5/2 cm × 2 cm × 3 cm. Sketch the dimensions.

Calculate fractional volume · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: fractional edge, cubic measure. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A 3/2×2×5 cm prism has volume 15 cm³; fractional lengths do not change the multiplication rule.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find volume of 1/2×6×4 m prism.
  2. Find volume of 3/4×4×2 cm prism.
  3. Compare 2.5×2×3 cm to 5/2×2×3 cm.
  4. Explain why multiplying a cubic measure by length does not produce square units.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

12 m³; 6 cm³; both 15 cm³; three lengths multiplied produce cubic units.

Wednesday — Find an unknown dimension

If volume and two dimensions are known, divide: a box has volume 120 cm³, length 6 cm and width 5 cm; height = 120 ÷ (6 × 5) = 4 cm. Substitution verifies 6 × 5 × 4 = 120.

Practice: volume 96 cm³, length 8 cm, width 3 cm. Find height. Explain why knowing only volume does not determine a unique set of dimensions.

Recover missing dimensions · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: dimension, inverse, verify. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: V=144 cm³, length=8 cm, width=6 cm: height=144/(8×6)=3 cm.

Independent reasoning:

  1. A 180 cm³ box has a 9×5 cm base. Find height.
  2. A 48 m³ box has length 4 m and height 3 m. Find width.
  3. Explain why V alone cannot identify every side length.
  4. Check a 2×3×7 prism using two different multiplications.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

4 cm; 4 m; many triples share same product; 2×3×7=42 and 2×(3×7)=42.

Thursday — Composite models and design limits

A model can be split into two nonoverlapping prisms. A 6 × 4 × 2 cm base plus a 2 × 2 × 3 cm raised section has total volume 48 + 12 = 60 cm³, assuming no overlap. A real storage box has wall thickness, packing gaps, and constraints not included in an ideal mathematical model.

Practice: combine 5 × 3 × 2 cm and 2 × 2 × 2 cm prisms without overlap. Compare capacity and surface area; explain why they answer different design questions.

Combine nonoverlapping solids · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: composite prism, overlap, capacity. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A 4×3×2 base plus a 2×2×3 block, not overlapping, yields 24+12=36 cm³.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Add volumes of nonoverlapping 5×2×3 and 3×2×2 prisms.
  2. Explain why overlapping solids require subtraction of overlap.
  3. Compare a 6×4×2 box with a 4×4×3 box by volume.
  4. Explain why packaging capacity may be less than external geometric volume.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

42 cm³; overlap would be counted twice; both 48 cubic units; wall thickness, objects and packing gaps.

Friday — Independent evidence

A (2 points): compute volume of 7 × 3 × 2 cm. B (2 points): compute volume of 3/2 × 4 × 2 cm. C (3 points): a 90 cm³ box is 5 cm long and 3 cm wide; find height and verify. D (3 points): model two joined nonoverlapping prisms of volumes 4 × 3 × 2 and 2 × 2 × 3, then defend the total and describe a limitation.

Teacher-reviewed practice is worth 10 points, with 8/10 indicating 80% formative achievement, but it never writes verified learner scores. The separate formal assessment determines progression.

Integrate volume and retention · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: estimate, capacity, model. Define the terms and use one in a mathematical explanation.

Worked model: A tank 2×3×4 m has geometric volume 24 m³. Capacity estimates need assumptions about wall thickness and usable fill.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find volume of a 3/2×4×6 cm prism.
  2. A 96 cm³ prism has a 4×6 cm base. Find height.
  3. Recall Week 12: find area of triangle base 10 height 7.
  4. Recall Week 11: mean of 2,4,6,8.
  5. Recall Week 10: solve x−3≤5.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

36 cm³; 4 cm; 35 cm²; mean 5; x≤8.
