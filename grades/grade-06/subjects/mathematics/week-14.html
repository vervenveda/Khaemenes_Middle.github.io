Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 · Mathematics · Week 14

Matter: Measurement, Ratios, and Models

How can measurements help us evaluate simplified models of matter?

Learning goals

Calculate mass-per-volume unit rates; build proportional tables; graph ordered pairs with labeled axes; compare evidence; and state the assumptions of a mathematical model. The physical-science theme provides context, but these lessons assess mathematics rather than unverified scientific identification.

Monday — Measure and organize

Mass is measured in grams and volume in cubic centimeters for these examples. A fictional sample has mass 24 g and volume 8 cm³. The ratio 24:8 simplifies to 3:1, and its mass per unit volume is 3 g/cm³. Distinguish the quantity, its measurement unit, and the model.

Try: 30 g in 10 cm³; 45 g in 15 cm³. Find both unit rates and explain why equal values do not establish that the samples are the same material.

Units and mass–volume ratios · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: mass, volume, unit rate. Define and use each term with an original example.

Worked model: A model sample has 54 g mass and 18 cm³ volume: 54 ÷ 18 = 3 g/cm³. Equal ratios describe a rate, not proof that samples are identical.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find the mass-per-volume rate of 56 g and 14 cm³.
  2. Find the rate for 72 g and 24 cm³.
  3. Explain why 3 g/cm³ and 3 cm³/g are not interchangeable.
  4. At 5 g/cm³, predict the mass of 8 cm³.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

4 g/cm³; 3 g/cm³; one is mass per volume, the other volume per mass; 40 g.

Tuesday — Tables and equivalent ratios

For a hypothetical uniform sample at 4 g/cm³, the mass–volume table is (volume, mass): (1,4), (2,8), (3,12), (5,20). The quantities form equivalent ratios only under the assumed constant property. This is a numerical model, not a claim that all real samples behave alike.

Try: fill a table for a sample at 6 g/cm³ for volumes 1, 2, 4, 7 cm³; explain how multiplying both terms maintains the rate.

Build proportional tables · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: ratio, constant, scale factor. Define and use each term with an original example.

Worked model: In the ideal model m = 2.5v, volumes 0,2,4,6 cm³ correspond to masses 0,5,10,15 g. The unit rate stays 2.5 g/cm³.

Independent reasoning:

  1. For m=6v find mass at v=1,3,5 cm³.
  2. Find volume when mass is 42 g at 6 g/cm³.
  3. Determine whether pairs (2,8),(4,16),(6,25) are proportional.
  4. Explain the assumption behind using one constant rate.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

6,18,30 g; 7 cm³; no, last ratio 25/6 differs from 4; homogeneous sample/unchanging proportional model.

Wednesday — Graph the evidence

Put volume (cm³) along the horizontal x-axis and mass (g) along the vertical y-axis. Plot (1,4), (2,8), (3,12), (5,20). In this ideal model the points fall on a line through the origin; the slope represents four grams per cubic centimeter. A graph should have descriptive axis labels and a consistent scale.

Try: for mass = 3 × volume, plot (0,0), (2,6), (4,12) and explain what (4,12) means in its units.

Interpret the slope and axes · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: ordered pair, slope, axis. Define and use each term with an original example.

Worked model: If x is volume cm³ and y is mass g, points (0,0),(2,10),(4,20) have slope 5 g/cm³. Reversing axes changes slope units.

Independent reasoning:

  1. For (0,0),(3,12),(6,24), find the rate with units.
  2. Describe the meaning of (5,15) on mass versus volume axes.
  3. If mass is 18 g at 6 cm³, find the proportional equation.
  4. Explain why a chart with unequal x spacing can mislead.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

4 g/cm³; 5 cm³ corresponds to 15 g; m = 3v; distorted spacing misrepresents numeric differences.

Thursday — Compare claims and limitations

Fictional sample A has mass 36 g and volume 9 cm³: 4 g/cm³. Sample B has mass 30 g and volume 10 cm³: 3 g/cm³. A learner says B is denser because 30 is a big mass. Compare unit rates instead of totals. Mention measurement uncertainty, nonuniformity, and the need to avoid identification of real substances from a simplified model.

Try: compare 28 g / 7 cm³ and 27 g / 9 cm³. Write a claim backed by two computations and identify one limitation.

Assess evidence and uncertainty · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: measurement, precision, assumption. Define and use each term with an original example.

Worked model: Sample A: 45 g/9 cm³ = 5 g/cm³; Sample B: 48 g/12 cm³ = 4 g/cm³. Different rates are observable, but these simplified numbers do not identify actual substances.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Compare 72 g/18 cm³ with 63 g/9 cm³.
  2. A mass measure is rounded to the nearest gram. Why should the result not imply unlimited precision?
  3. Which is more informative for comparison, total mass or g/cm³?
  4. Recall Week 13: find volume of a 3×4×5 cm prism.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

4 versus 7 g/cm³, B greater; measurement uncertainty limits precision; rate is comparable; 60 cm³.

Friday — Formative mastery evidence

A (2 points): calculate 42 g / 7 cm³ with units. B (2 points): find mass at 5 cm³ for a constant 3 g/cm³ model. C (3 points): graph the points (1,2), (2,4), (4,8) with axes labeled, and interpret the ratio. D (3 points): compare 48 g / 12 cm³ and 35 g / 7 cm³, defend a conclusion, and explain what the model cannot prove.

Synthesize mathematical modeling · Deep practice

Vocabulary studio: justify, ratio, model. Define and use each term with an original example.

Worked model: At a constant 3.5 g/cm³, 8 cm³ is predicted to weigh 28 g. The prediction depends on a valid uniform-sample model.

Independent reasoning:

  1. Find the unit rate of 84 g and 12 cm³.
  2. At 4 g/cm³ predict mass for 9 cm³.
  3. Graph mass points (0,0),(2,6),(4,12) and state slope.
  4. Recall Week 12: surface area of a closed 2×3×4 cm prism.
  5. Recall Week 11: median of 3,6,8,10.
Teacher solution key (open after attempting)

7 g/cm³; 36 g; 3 g/cm³; 52 cm²; 7.

Answer key

Reveal after independent practice
