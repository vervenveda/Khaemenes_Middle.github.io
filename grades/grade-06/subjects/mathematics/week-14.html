Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 · Mathematics · Week 14

Matter: Measurement, Ratios, and Models

How can measurements help us evaluate simplified models of matter?

Learning goals

Calculate mass-per-volume unit rates; build proportional tables; graph ordered pairs with labeled axes; compare evidence; and state the assumptions of a mathematical model. The physical-science theme provides context, but these lessons assess mathematics rather than unverified scientific identification.

Monday — Measure and organize

Mass is measured in grams and volume in cubic centimeters for these examples. A fictional sample has mass 24 g and volume 8 cm³. The ratio 24:8 simplifies to 3:1, and its mass per unit volume is 3 g/cm³. Distinguish the quantity, its measurement unit, and the model.

Try: 30 g in 10 cm³; 45 g in 15 cm³. Find both unit rates and explain why equal values do not establish that the samples are the same material.

Tuesday — Tables and equivalent ratios

For a hypothetical uniform sample at 4 g/cm³, the mass–volume table is (volume, mass): (1,4), (2,8), (3,12), (5,20). The quantities form equivalent ratios only under the assumed constant property. This is a numerical model, not a claim that all real samples behave alike.

Try: fill a table for a sample at 6 g/cm³ for volumes 1, 2, 4, 7 cm³; explain how multiplying both terms maintains the rate.

Wednesday — Graph the evidence

Put volume (cm³) along the horizontal x-axis and mass (g) along the vertical y-axis. Plot (1,4), (2,8), (3,12), (5,20). In this ideal model the points fall on a line through the origin; the slope represents four grams per cubic centimeter. A graph should have descriptive axis labels and a consistent scale.

Try: for mass = 3 × volume, plot (0,0), (2,6), (4,12) and explain what (4,12) means in its units.

Thursday — Compare claims and limitations

Fictional sample A has mass 36 g and volume 9 cm³: 4 g/cm³. Sample B has mass 30 g and volume 10 cm³: 3 g/cm³. A learner says B is denser because 30 is a big mass. Compare unit rates instead of totals. Mention measurement uncertainty, nonuniformity, and the need to avoid identification of real substances from a simplified model.

Try: compare 28 g / 7 cm³ and 27 g / 9 cm³. Write a claim backed by two computations and identify one limitation.

Friday — Formative mastery evidence

A (2 points): calculate 42 g / 7 cm³ with units. B (2 points): find mass at 5 cm³ for a constant 3 g/cm³ model. C (3 points): graph the points (1,2), (2,4), (4,8) with axes labeled, and interpret the ratio. D (3 points): compare 48 g / 12 cm³ and 35 g / 7 cm³, defend a conclusion, and explain what the model cannot prove.

Eight out of ten points meets this formative lesson benchmark. Only the separately saved Academy examination verifies weekly 80% mastery and progression.

Answer key

Reveal after independent practice

Monday: both 3 g/cm³. Tuesday: masses 6, 12, 24, 42 grams. Wednesday: (4,12) means 12 g for 4 cm³; rate 3 g/cm³. Thursday: 4 g/cm³ versus 3 g/cm³. Friday A: 6 g/cm³; B: 15 g; C: rate 2 g/cm³; D: 4 g/cm³ versus 5 g/cm³, so the second rate is larger in the ideal model.
