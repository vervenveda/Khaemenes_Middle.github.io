Khaemenes Academy · Grade 6 Mathematics · Week 10

Inequalities and Decision Rules

How do constraints shape solutions and decisions?

Learning targets

Read and graph inequalities on a number line; distinguish strict and inclusive boundaries; solve one-step inequalities with signed values; reverse direction when multiplying or dividing by a negative number; and model practical limits with reasonable units. Vocabulary: inequality, solution set, boundary, constraint, feasible value, open circle, closed circle. All essential instruction is included for offline study.

Monday — Compare quantities and represent solution sets

An inequality compares values: < means less than; > greater than; ≤ less than or equal; ≥ greater than or equal. For x ≤ 4, the solutions include 4 and all smaller numbers. On a number line, mark a closed circle at 4 and shade left. For x > −2, use an open circle at −2 and shade right.

Practice: (1) interpret y ≥ 6 in words and graph it; (2) choose whether 4 satisfies x < 4 and explain; (3) distinguish ≤ from < using a practical threshold.

Tuesday — Solve addition and subtraction inequalities

The inverse-operation rule works as it does for equations when adding or subtracting: x + 5 < 13 means x < 8. For n − 7 ≥ −2, add 7 to both sides: n ≥ 5. Verify by testing the boundary and values on both sides.

Practice: (4) solve x − 4 ≤ 9; (5) solve t + 6 > −1; (6) test whether x = 12 satisfies x − 4 ≤ 9.

Wednesday — Solve multiplication and division inequalities

Dividing or multiplying by a positive value preserves direction: 3p ≥ 18 gives p ≥ 6. Dividing or multiplying by a negative value reverses the symbol: −2x < 10 yields x > −5. Check by substituting −4, which makes 8 < 10 true, and −6, which makes 12 < 10 false.

Practice: (7) solve 4z > 20; (8) solve −3r ≥ 12; (9) explain why the inequality reverses when multiplied by −1 using −2 < 1 becoming 2 > −1.

Thursday — Real-world constraints and fair decisions

A fictional garden club has at most $48 for supplies. A starter kit costs $12 and each planting tray costs $6. Write 12 + 6t ≤ 48. Subtract 12 and divide by 6: t ≤ 6. Since trays are counted in whole numbers, the feasible choices are t = 0,1,2,3,4,5,6. Test seven trays: $54 exceeds the budget.

Practice: (10) a room holds no more than 35 participants and 12 are registered; write and solve 12 + p ≤ 35; (11) explain why fractional participants are inappropriate; (12) compare an age requirement of 12 or older with an age requirement greater than 12.

Friday — Explain, defend and review mastery

A (2 points): Graph x > 3, marking the boundary correctly. B (2 points): Solve n − 5 ≤ 9 and test a boundary value. C (3 points): Solve −4m ≥ 20; explain the reversed inequality symbol. D (3 points): A group has $70; travel costs $10 plus $5 per person. Write an inequality, identify whole-number feasible group sizes, and evaluate a claim that 13 people can attend.

Teacher-reviewed work totals 10 points. At least 8/10 indicates 80% mastery of this formative task, but the separate authorized examination alone writes verified scores or unlocks curriculum. For remediation, redraw number-line boundaries, practice inverse operations, and check a true and false value.

Answer key and explanations

Reveal after completing the practice

1. At least 6, closed circle at 6 shaded right. 2. No, strict less-than excludes 4. 3. Inclusive threshold allows equality. 4. x ≤ 13. 5. t > −7. 6. Yes, 8 ≤ 9. 7. z > 5. 8. r ≤ −4. 9. Multiplication by negative reverses number-line order. 10. p ≤ 23 participants. 11. Participants are whole people. 12. “12 or older” is age ≥ 12, but “greater than 12” is age > 12. Friday A: open 3 and shade right. B: n ≤ 14 and n = 14 satisfies. C: m ≤ −5; divide by −4 and reverse the sign. D: 10 + 5p ≤ 70 yields p ≤ 12 whole people; 13 would cost $75 and exceed the budget.
